# Part 20 — Condensed case study: a year in a store's life

Here, in condensed form, is how these principles fit together over time in a fictional store followed over twelve months — the goal isn't to describe a specific real case, but to show the method in motion rather than topic by topic in isolation.

Month 1: a cautious launch with a single product chosen using the method in Part 2, a supplier compared on reliability, an ad budget split into tiers. Month 2: a supplier announces a longer delivery time — the store communicates proactively before customers worry, and identifies a second backup supplier in reserve. Months 3-4: two coherent complementary products are added, a bundle offer is tested and improves the margin per customer with no extra acquisition cost. Month 5: a stock-out at the main supplier is absorbed thanks to the already qualified backup supplier and the cash reserve already built up. Month 6: revenue approaches a threshold that warrants checking the tax regime with an accountant, anticipated rather than discovered too late.

Months 7-9: the ad budget increases gradually with constant tracking of acquisition cost relative to margin; an erosion of the conversion rate on the main page is spotted through a regular audit and corrected. Month 10: a dispute occurs, and regular tracking reveals that it's part of a slight rise in the refund rate on a specific product over several weeks — the cause, an imprecise description, is corrected immediately. Months 11-12: rather than accelerating unchecked, the end of the year consolidates what works — documenting the successful product choices, revising the safety reserve upward, first thoughts about a stronger brand identity.

None of these situations is exceptional on its own. What distinguishes this store from one that would have closed within the year is that each problem was anticipated upfront or handled quickly thanks to regular tracking, rather than discovered late — the central thesis of this course, from start to finish.

# Part 21 — Essential glossary

Net margin: what remains after deducting all real costs, as opposed to gross margin, which often deducts only the product cost. Customer acquisition cost (CAC): the average amount spent on advertising to get one paying customer. Cross-selling: offering a complementary product at the time of purchase, which improves the margin with no extra acquisition cost. Return: the customer physically sending a product back. Refund: giving the money back, with or without a physical return. Dispute (chargeback): a challenge of the payment made directly with the customer's bank, judged by a third party, the most serious of the three. Micro-enterprise: a French simplified regime with contributions calculated on revenue collected. IOSS: a European scheme simplifying VAT on low-value shipments. Loss leader: a low-priced product used to attract a first purchase, often with a reduced unit margin. Backup supplier: a second qualified supplier, not actively used, ready to take over if the main supplier fails.
