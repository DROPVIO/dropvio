# Dropshipping Course — Short version
## Part 1 — Market reality, unfiltered

Dropshipping attracts with its starting promise: no stock to finance, no warehouse to manage, a business that can start on a limited budget. That promise is real, but it comes with a reality that few contents selling quick courses highlight with the same honesty: most stores launched don't make it past their first year. The exact figure varies by source and no one has a perfectly reliable primary study on the subject — you can keep in mind, as an approximation widely repeated in the industry rather than as verified data, a failure rate counted in tens of percent from the first year. This figure isn't meant to discourage, but to frame honestly what follows: this business rewards method, not improvisation.

What separates the stores that last from those that close is almost never luck or a miracle product found nowhere else. It's the rigor applied to a few simple but rarely consistently respected disciplines: calculating a real margin before launching a campaign, checking a supplier's reliability before committing, keeping a cash reserve, treating after-sales service as a priority and not an administrative chore. This condensed course covers each of these disciplines, aiming to give you, in a fraction of the long version's volume, the benchmarks that matter most to start and last.

One of the most common traps for beginner sellers is copying a method that worked for someone else without understanding why it worked in that precise context. A product that worked well for another seller, in a market they've probably already saturated by talking about it publicly, often no longer has the same opportunity by the time you discover it. What you should copy isn't someone else's product, it's the method that let them find it and exploit it properly.

## Part 2 — Choosing a product with method

A good dropshipping product is recognized by four combined criteria, never by one alone. It sits in an intermediate search volume zone: neither so rare that demand is uncertain, nor so massive that competition is already established and expensive to face. Its trend over time, observed over several months rather than at a single moment, must distinguish underlying growth from a simple fad spike bound to fall as fast. Its price must leave a comfortable margin once all real costs are deducted, not just the raw purchase cost. And it must meet a need or desire clear enough to be explained in one sentence — a product that has to be justified at length before the customer understands its appeal rarely converts well.

Checking the supplier must come before any commitment, never after. Compare several potential suppliers for the same product on reliability criteria — product rating, volume of orders already placed — rather than committing to the first result in a search engine. A test order before any volume launch remains the only way to check what no public data shows: the real quality received, the real delivery time, the quality of the packaging.

Finally, a product chosen in line with an existing or planned range is almost always worth more than a spectacular but isolated product, because it opens the door to the cross-selling detailed later in this course — one of the most underused profitability levers among beginner sellers.

## Part 3 — Pricing and margins: the calculation that decides everything

Too many sellers set a selling price by simply looking at what their competitors do, without having first calculated what that price really leaves them once all costs are deducted. The complete calculation must include, in order: the real product cost at the supplier, shipping included; the payment processor's transaction fees, generally around 1.5% plus a fixed amount per transaction — a fixed amount that weighs proportionally more on a low-priced product, a common trap with loss leaders; import duties and taxes, including the European flat tax of 3 euros per item category applied since July 1, 2026 to parcels under 150 euros coming from outside the EU; the advertising acquisition cost, to be estimated prudently then checked against real data; an assumed return rate consistent with the product category, never assumed to be zero; and finally the social contributions or taxes applicable to your status, calculated on revenue collected and not on margin.

Let's take a tight example: an accessory with a supplier cost of 3.20 euros sold for 14.90 euros. After transaction fees (about 0.47 euro), the European tax on a parcel shipped from outside the EU (3 euros), an estimated acquisition cost of 4 euros, and an 8% return rate that brings the real average margin down to about 3.89 euros, then URSSAF contributions as a micro-enterprise at about 12.3% of revenue collected (about 1.83 euros here), what remains is an approximate net margin of 2.06 euros per sale, a little under 14% of the selling price — barely positive, and tipping into the red at the slightest rise in acquisition cost. This kind of complete calculation, redone for each product before each campaign launch, is the difference between a controlled launch and a blind one.

Bundling, pairing a main product with a lower-cost complement, often improves the margin disproportionately to the extra effort it requires: the acquisition cost stays the same for a customer who buys both products together, while the margin increases significantly — one of the most profitable levers in the whole business, precisely because it adds almost nothing to the most expensive item in the calculation, acquisition.

## Part 4 — Building a store that converts

An effective product page answers the visitor's questions before they ask them, rather than being limited to a list of technical specs copied from the supplier's listing. The first image should show the product in a real usage context, not just isolated on a white background. The copy should say who the product is for, in what situation it solves a problem, and honestly acknowledge a minor limitation rather than presenting only perfect advantages — a page that's too perfect arouses the distrust of a visitor used to online marketing.

The total price must be visible with no hidden calculation, and reassurance elements — delivery time, return policy, payment methods — must be placed near the buy button, exactly where doubt appears, rather than relegated to the bottom of the page where few visitors actually look. These principles, inspired by the public work of the Nielsen Norman Group and the Baymard Institute on the online shopping experience, are precisely what Dropvio's built-in landing page auditor checks automatically — a score out of a hundred across nine criteria, mobile version included — before you send expensive ad traffic to a page that would still have gaps.

Complementary products offered as cross-sells must be chosen for their real coherence with the main product, never added at random to artificially inflate the cart — a coherence the customer perceives as forced hurts conversion more than it helps.

## Part 5 — Suppliers and logistics: the invisible foundation

An excellent product paired with an unreliable supplier almost always ends up producing a store in difficulty, because it's the supplier, not the product, that determines the real delivery time, the quality observed on receipt, and stock availability over time — three elements directly responsible for customer satisfaction and therefore for the rate of returns and disputes. The average rating and volume of orders already placed through a supplier give a first indication of reliability, but with an honest limit to know: a complete history of seniority or past disputes remains out of reach of the public tools available to an independent seller.

That's why a test order before any volume launch, and identifying a second backup supplier for any product likely to become significant in revenue, remain the two most effective precautions — they only cost a little time upfront, and avoid dependence on a single point of failure that can shut down a large part of the business if a sudden problem hits the main supplier.
