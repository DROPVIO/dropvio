# Part 28 — Handling a supplier or financial crisis without panicking

Despite all the precautions detailed in this course, a crisis almost always ends up happening in one form or another — a sudden stock-out, a supplier abruptly deteriorating, a particularly tight cash month. This part gives a method for reacting in those moments, rather than improvising under pressure.

## The first reaction: assess before deciding

Faced with a crisis signal — a supplier announcing a prolonged stock-out, a sudden rise in refunds on a product, cash tightening faster than expected — the most useful reflex isn't to immediately decide on a radical action, but to take a few hours to precisely measure the real extent of the problem from the available data: what share of revenue does this product or supplier really represent, what's the exact trend rather than an impression, how much time remains before the situation becomes critical. This measurement step, which rarely takes more than an hour, avoids two symmetrical mistakes: under-reacting by hoping the problem solves itself, or over-reacting by making a radical, costly decision on an incomplete basis.

## Activate the precautions already taken, rather than reinventing everything in a rush

It's precisely in these moments that the precautions detailed elsewhere in this course make full sense: the backup supplier already qualified for this product, the cash reserve already built up, regular tracking that lets you measure the real extent of a rise in disputes rather than guess it. A store that followed these recommendations upfront handles a crisis in a few days with options already ready; a store that discovers at that exact moment that it has neither a backup supplier nor a reserve has to improvise in a rush, with a much higher risk of error.

## Communicate before silence makes things worse

Faced with a problem affecting customers who have already ordered (longer delay, product temporarily unavailable), communicating quickly and honestly, following the proactive transparency logic detailed in the part on customer service, almost always limits the damage compared with a silence that leaves the customer feeling ignored. This communication should include a realistic estimate of the resolution time, even if it means revising it later, rather than a total absence of information that pushes the customer toward worry, then a complaint, or even a dispute.

## Document the crisis once resolved, don't just get through it

Once the situation is stabilized, taking the time to note what happened, what made it manageable, and what could have been anticipated earlier turns a one-off crisis into reusable learning for the future rather than a bad memory quickly forgotten. It's this capitalization, more than the crisis itself, that distinguishes a store that gradually becomes more resilient from one that goes through each new crisis with the same level of preparation as the last.
