# Dropshipping Course — Short version (continued)
## Part 6 — Acquisition and marketing

The riskiest reflex at launch is to immediately commit a large ad budget, carried by the enthusiasm of starting. A more prudent approach splits the test into successive tiers, each reassessed before moving on to the next, rather than drawing conclusions from a data volume still too small to be reliable — cutting a campaign after a few hours on a disappointing result, or on the contrary betting everything on it after an exceptional first result, are two symmetrical mistakes made on too small a sample.

An acquisition cost is never judged in absolute terms: it must always be related to the available margin calculated for that specific product in Part 3. The same acquisition cost can be excellent on a high-margin product and catastrophic on a low-margin one. Depending on a single acquisition channel, even when it works well, exposes the whole business to any unfavorable change in that channel — an algorithm change, a general rise in costs, an account suspension sometimes for a vague reason. Diversifying gradually, once a first channel has proven viable, reduces this risk without prematurely scattering a still-limited budget.

## Part 7 — Customer service, returns and disputes

A store's lasting reputation is decided after the sale, not before. A customer whose problem was handled well often becomes more loyal than a customer whose order simply went smoothly, because they could verify the seller's seriousness concretely. Faced with a delay, warning the customer before they worry completely changes perception compared with a silent wait followed by a complaint.

Three situations must be clearly distinguished: a return is physically sending a product back; a refund is giving the money back, whether or not it follows a return; a dispute is a challenge made directly with the customer's bank, the most serious situation since it's judged by a third party. A dispute rate above a certain threshold — around 1% of transactions depending on the provider, to be taken as an approximate benchmark rather than a universal rule — can trigger up to suspension of the payment account, an existential risk for an online store. The best prevention remains transparency and responsiveness upstream, which make a dispute rarely necessary for a customer whose legitimate request gets a quick answer.

Keep a centralized overview of your refunds and disputes, with an honest distinction between "no dispute recorded" and "data not available for this store" depending on the payment method used — a nuance that avoids confusing a zero figure with a real absence of possible tracking.

## Part 8 — Finances and cash flow: staying stable

A store can be profitable on paper and still find itself unable to pay a bill, simply because the money that should come in hasn't arrived yet when the money has to go out. Keeping a reserve equivalent to several weeks of fixed costs, rather than considering that all available money can be reinvested immediately in advertising, protects against months when sales slow down or several refunds arrive at the same time.

Separating personal money from business money, even as a micro-enterprise where the legal structure doesn't strictly require it, clarifies the reading of real profitability. Setting aside social contributions as soon as money comes in, rather than waiting for the deadline to improvise, avoids turning a perfectly predictable deadline into a bad surprise. Finally, steering the business on regularly tracked indicators — real net margin, acquisition cost relative to that margin, return rate — rather than on a general impression, lets you spot in time that a seemingly high-performing product is gradually becoming loss-making.

## Part 9 — Legal and tax framework in France

*These guidelines are general and don't replace an accountant's advice on your specific situation — thresholds and obligations change regularly.*

The micro-enterprise remains the most common starting point for going solo, with contributions calculated on revenue collected rather than a lump sum due even without sales. It does, however, have limits to know from the start: a revenue ceiling, a VAT exemption that disappears above a certain threshold, and no possibility of deducting real costs. A company (EURL, SASU) can become more relevant once the business has grown.

VAT has become more complex in recent years: the general rule now is to apply it to the total price charged to the customer when selling to consumers, not just to the margin. The IOSS scheme simplifies this collection for low-value shipments, sparing the customer a bad customs surprise. Since July 1, 2026, a European flat tax of 3 euros per item category also applies to parcels under 150 euros coming from outside the EU, to be included in the margin calculation. Beyond taxation, a store must display terms and conditions of sale, a GDPR-compliant privacy policy, clear information on the right of withdrawal, and a legal notice — documents that protect as much as they constrain, particularly in a dispute with a customer. Finally, obtaining a SIRET number and fully activating a payment solution take administrative time that can't be compressed, to be anticipated several weeks before any planned launch.

## Part 10 — Scaling and sustaining your business

Scaling doesn't simply mean spending more on advertising hoping results follow proportionally — it means first solidifying what works before amplifying it. Diversifying the range with coherent complementary products spreads risk rather than depending on a single star product. Many sellers who build a lasting business gradually evolve from generic dropshipping toward something that looks like a brand — custom packaging, a tight selection, a recognizable identity — precisely to answer the difficulty of standing out when anyone can sell the same product.

Some disciplines must stay intact whatever scale is reached: calculating the margin before each new product, tracking cash flow, paying attention to after-sales service, staying vigilant about supplier reliability. The clearest difference between a business that lasts and one that collapses rarely comes down to an isolated dramatic event — it almost always comes down to the ability to spot weak signals before they become major problems: a return rate creeping up, a margin gradually eroding, a supplier whose quality declines before the first real disaster. Dropshipping is neither the sure-fire system some sell, nor the model doomed to failure that others systematically criticize — it's a business that rewards rigor and a clear-eyed view of the numbers, more than any isolated tactic.
