# Part 31 — Anticipating seasonality rather than suffering it

Some products behave predictably from one year to the next, at similar periods — true seasonality, distinct both from a lasting underlying trend and from a one-off fad spike mentioned in the part on choosing a product. A strongly seasonal product can be an excellent commercial choice, provided you correctly anticipate the high and low periods rather than discovering them each cycle as a surprise.

A supplier faced with a sudden rise in demand as a recognized seasonal peak approaches can see its delivery times lengthen significantly, simply because its logistics capacity is being called on by many sellers at once — checking real delivery times several weeks before the peak, rather than at the peak itself, reduces this risk. Advertising acquisition cost itself varies by period, generally upward when many sellers compete for the attention of the same buyers — a margin calculation must be revised as these periods approach rather than keeping an assumption established at a quieter time of year.

Periods of strong seasonality also generate more customer service requests, especially about delivery times — preparing replies suited to that specific period in advance avoids improvising in a rush when the volume of messages is highest. Conversely, quiet periods shouldn't be experienced as downtime, but as a window to prepare the next period: researching new products, enriching existing pages, consolidating cash before the next busy period.

# Part 32 — A third worked example: the effect of a returning customer

The previous parts quantified the effect of cross-selling on a single order. Here's how a second purchase, a few weeks later, changes the overall calculation for the same customer acquired once.

Let's take the niche product from Appendix C: a supplier cost of 9.50 euros, sold for 34.90 euros, for a net margin of about 6.82 euros on the first purchase, with an acquisition cost of 9 euros and the European tax of 3 euros already deducted. If this same customer comes back a month later for a second purchase of a complementary product with a comparable margin, this second purchase bears no extra acquisition cost — the customer is already acquired. Using the same assumptions for transaction fees, return rate and contributions on this second purchase, the net margin on this second sale is around 14.74 euros, more than double the first, precisely because the most expensive item in the first calculation, acquisition, disappears entirely.

Out of a hundred customers acquired, if only a third come back for a second purchase of this kind — a modest and deliberately prudent retention rate for this example — the total margin generated by this second cycle of purchases, with no additional acquisition cost, already represents more than two thirds of the margin generated by all of the first hundred purchases. It's this mechanism, more than any abstract talk about the importance of retention, that justifies with numbers why investing in the relationship with an already acquired customer remains one of the most profitable levers in the whole business.
