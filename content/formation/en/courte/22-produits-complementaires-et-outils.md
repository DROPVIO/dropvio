# Part 22 — Building a complementary range that sells itself

A product can complement another in three distinct ways, not to be confused. Functional complementarity concerns an accessory needed to use the main product — it's the one that converts best as an immediate cross-sell, because its relevance is obvious to the customer without further explanation. Usage complementarity concerns products used in the same context without direct functional dependence. Upgrade complementarity concerns a higher version offered after a first, more modest purchase, once a base of loyal customers already exists.

Rather than guessing in advance which complementary products will work, the questions most frequently asked of customer service about using the main product often reveal an unmet need that a specific product would satisfy. The opposite mistake, just as common, is adding products simply because they're promising in isolation, without checking their coherence with the rest of the range — a store with a disparate catalog loses positioning readability and complicates cross-selling since no natural offer emerges. Gradual construction, in concentric circles of decreasing complementarity starting from a solid main product, lets you check at each step that the addition really strengthens the range.

The most useful test before any addition: would a customer who has already bought the main product immediately understand, without complex explanation, why this new product is being offered to them? If the answer requires a long justification, the product probably isn't complementary in the sense that matters commercially.

# Part 23 — Where each Dropvio feature fits into the method

When searching for and validating a product: the Finder, which queries the AliExpress catalog through its official API and tracks the daily trend of order volume, before comparing several suppliers on reliability — product rating, volume of orders already placed — before any commitment. When calculating a margin: the built-in calculator, which follows exactly the logic worked through in Part 3 — product cost, transaction fees, import taxes, acquisition, returns, contributions — in the mode for the country you sell to (ten countries, plus a manual international mode), to avoid forgetting a cost item under launch pressure. When building a product page: the landing page auditor, which scores the page out of a hundred points across nine criteria, including the mobile version, before an ad budget is committed to it. When building the store: the site builder, for a single-product page or a multi-product store from styles and palettes, with a mobile preview and export to Shopify; a connected Shopify store receives the products and keeps orders and tracking numbers in sync. Once the business is running, for stores connected through Shopify: keep regular track of refunds and disputes, with an honest distinction between a real absence of incidents and data simply not available depending on the payment method used.

None of these tools replaces the method itself: a margin calculator is useless if its result is ignored out of enthusiasm, and an incident dashboard is useless if no one checks it regularly. These tools reduce the workload and the risk of error in an already solid method; they never replace the discipline of applying it.
