# Part 24 — Two more margin examples, to anchor the method

Appendix C has already worked through a complete calculation on a niche product. Here are two additional, shorter examples, to cover different product profiles and help you reproduce this reasoning whatever your own price positioning.

## The low-priced loss leader

An accessory with a supplier cost of 3.20 euros, sold for 14.90 euros. Transaction fees (about 1.5% + 0.25 euro) represent a proportionally heavier share here than on a more expensive product, because of the fixed amount — the classic loss-leader trap, made worse by the European tax of 3 euros on a parcel coming from outside the EU, 20% of the selling price on its own. With an estimated acquisition cost of 4 euros and an 8% return rate, the real average margin before contributions falls to around 3.89 euros, then about 2.06 euros net once URSSAF contributions are deducted (12.3% of revenue collected) — a little under 14% of the selling price. This kind of product hardly justifies itself on its own anymore: only if it triggers a complementary sale or a second purchase, and if the real acquisition cost is confirmed in practice, never assumed in advance.

## The premium bundle

A main product costing 11 euros, paired with a complementary accessory costing 2.50 euros, sold together for 59.90 euros. The acquisition cost stays the same as for a single sale, since it's the same customer acquired once — the direct advantage of cross-selling. After transaction fees, the European tax on the parcel (two items in different categories, so prudently 2 × 3 euros), acquisition cost (9 euros) and a 10% return rate, the real average margin before contributions is around 27.23 euros; once contributions are deducted, the final net margin reaches about 19.86 euros per sale, about 33% of the selling price — far higher, in percentage as in absolute value, than the two previous examples, for an acquisition cost identical to that of a single sale. That's the numerical demonstration of why cross-selling remains one of the most profitable levers in the whole business.

## What these two examples confirm together

Fixed costs per order — the fixed amount of transaction fees, the tax per parcel — weigh all the more heavily the lower the selling price: that's what separates a barely profitable loss leader from a comfortable bundle offer. And comparing only margin percentages between products at very different prices can be misleading: depending on costs, a lower percentage on a more expensive product can represent a much higher absolute amount per sale than a high percentage on a loss leader. It's the real net amount per sale, related to the expected volume, that should guide what you highlight in the store — not the percentage shown on a spreadsheet alone.

# Part 25 — Completing a supplier evaluation

Beyond the average rating and order volume already compared, two additional checks deserve to be systematic before any volume commitment. First, a supplier's responsiveness and clarity when answering questions asked before committing — about delivery times, packaging customization, handling a stock problem — is an underestimated but highly predictive signal of their behavior once a real problem arises after launch: a supplier who is hard to reach before the first order is unlikely to suddenly become responsive under the pressure of a real incident.

Next, for any product likely to become significant in revenue, qualifying at least one backup supplier, even without actively using them, considerably reduces the risk that a stock-out or sudden quality decline at the main supplier shuts down a large part of the business overnight — a precaution that only costs the initial qualification time.
