# Part 36 — The most common mistakes, phrased as direct warnings

This last part revisits the whole course from a different angle: rather than what to do, here's what to avoid, phrased directly, for those who remember a clear warning better than a positive recommendation.

Never launch an ad campaign without first calculating the product's complete net margin — a selling price that looks attractive on paper can become non-viable once all real costs are included. Never commit to a supplier on the basis of an attractive price alone without having compared their reliability with other candidates and without placing a test order.

Never pour a large ad budget in all at once at launch — test in tiers, and draw no definitive conclusion on a data volume still too small to be reliable, in either direction. Never leave a customer complaint unanswered for several days — a customer who doesn't get a quick answer turns more easily to a bank dispute, the most costly situation for your store.

Never reinvest all your revenue in advertising without keeping a cash reserve or setting aside your upcoming social contributions — a business that's profitable on paper can still collapse for lack of cash at the right moment. Never mix your personal finances with the business's, even as a micro-enterprise where nothing legally obliges you to separate them.

Never indefinitely postpone regularizing your business administratively by telling yourself you'll deal with it once it's profitable — an undeclared business remains illegal from the first euro of profit, whatever the amount. Never assume that a tax or legal rule learned at a given moment remains valid indefinitely, especially on VAT and import taxes, whose framework has changed a lot in recent years.

Never depend on a single supplier or a single acquisition channel for a large share of your revenue without at least having identified a backup alternative. Never neglect the very first customers of a new store on the pretext that volume is still low — each of these first experiences weighs proportionally more on the budding reputation than one sale among hundreds once the business is established.

Never relax, once the business is growing, the disciplines that got you to that level — systematic margin calculation, regular tracking of indicators, vigilance about supplier reliability. It's precisely under the pressure of volume that these disciplines are most tempting to sacrifice, and it's precisely at that moment that slacking costs the most.

Finally, never copy a product just because it worked for another seller, without understanding the method that made it relevant at that precise moment — a product already made public has often lost part of its opportunity by the time you discover it, while the method, for its part, remains transferable to any product you choose yourself with rigor.
