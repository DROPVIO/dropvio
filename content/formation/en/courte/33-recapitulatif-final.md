# Part 33 — Final recap: the complete method in one place

This last part brings together, step by step, all the checks detailed separately in the previous parts, so you can refer to them quickly without rereading the whole course for every new decision.

## Before choosing a product

Narrow a broad idea into concrete variants rather than keeping it vague. Check that search volume sits in an intermediate zone, neither negligible nor already saturated. Observe the trend over several months to distinguish underlying growth, predictable seasonality, or a simple fad spike. Compare at least two or three suppliers on their average rating and order volume. Check the product's coherence with the existing or planned range.

## Before validating a supplier

Place a test order before any volume commitment, to check real quality, real delivery time and packaging. Assess the responsiveness and clarity of communication before committing, not only after a problem. Identify a second backup supplier for any product likely to become significant.

## Before launching an ad campaign

Calculate the complete net margin — product cost, transaction fees, import duties and taxes, estimated acquisition cost, an assumed return rate consistent with the category, contributions on revenue — and check that it stays positive with a reasonable safety margin. Have the product page audited on conversion criteria before sending paid traffic to it. Split the test budget into tiers rather than committing it all at once. Choose a first channel consistent with the nature of the product rather than spreading the budget over several channels at once.

## Once the first sales are made

Handle each complaint quickly, acknowledging the problem before justifying it and offering a solution before the customer has to demand it. Give extra attention to the very first customers, whose every experience weighs proportionally more than the future average. Draw no definitive statistical conclusion on a data volume still too small, in either direction.

## On an ongoing basis, once the business is established

Hold a weekly review of key indicators — real margin, return rate, cash flow, acquisition cost relative to margin. Set aside social contributions as soon as money comes in rather than as the deadline approaches. Keep a reserve equivalent to several weeks of fixed costs. Separate personal money from business money without exception. Watch for weak signals — a gradual rise in the return rate, a gradual lengthening of a supplier's delivery time, slow erosion of a margin, slower customer service — before they become major problems.

## On the legal and tax side

Regularly check, with an accountant if needed, whether your status and VAT regime remain suited to the revenue level reached. Keep the terms and conditions of sale, privacy policy, legal notice and right-of-withdrawal information up to date. Anticipate administrative delays (SIRET, activating a payment solution) several weeks before any planned launch or change of status.

## In a crisis

Precisely measure the real extent of the problem before deciding on a radical action. Activate the precautions already taken — backup supplier, cash reserve — rather than improvising entirely in a rush. Communicate quickly and honestly with the customers concerned. Document the crisis once resolved to build on the experience.

## To grow the business over time

Expand the range with genuinely complementary products rather than ones added at random, checking with each addition that an already acquired customer would immediately understand the relevance of the offer. Gradually diversify acquisition channels once a first channel has proven viable. Invest in the relationship with already acquired customers, who generate a higher margin with no extra acquisition cost. Never relax, under the pressure of volume, the disciplines that got you to that volume in the first place — it's this consistency, more than any isolated tactic, that distinguishes a business that lasts from one that stops.
