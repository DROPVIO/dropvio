# Dropshipping Course — Short version (continued 3)

## Part 16 — Anatomy of a typical failure

A common failure pattern starts with an enthusiastic launch without a prior margin calculation, with a supplier chosen only for price without comparison. The ad budget is then poured in all at once rather than tested in tiers, and amplified on the basis of first results obtained on a data volume too small to be reliable. With no cash reserve built up, the slightest supplier incident blocks the business. Customer service, neglected under the pressure of acquisition, leaves complaints unanswered, which pushes several customers toward a bank dispute rather than a standard refund. The dispute rate then exceeds the payment provider's tolerance threshold, and it suspends the account — a total halt of the business. None of these steps is an isolated catastrophe: it's their chain that turns a promising launch into a closure, and each discipline detailed in this course aims to break that chain at its earliest possible link.

## Part 17 — Comparing ad channels

Highly visual social networks favor products that are understood immediately from an image, with interest-based targeting that optimizes progressively — a test budget that's too small or too short often prevents this optimization from reaching a meaningful stage. Search engines target an intent already expressed, better suited to products for which search demand already exists than to new products. Organic content and partnerships with creators reduce dependence on a paid budget but take time before producing measurable results. Concentrating a first test budget on a single channel, chosen according to the nature of the product, lets you reach an interpretable data volume faster — and whatever the channel, the acquisition cost must always be related to the product's margin, never interpreted alone.

## Part 18 — Understanding why a visitor buys, or doesn't

What drives a visitor away is often perceived friction more than real friction: a process that looks long discourages even if it's objectively fast. Uncertainty about the seller's reliability, whether the product matches, or how easy a return is often weighs more than the displayed price itself — it's this uncertainty that well-placed reassurance reduces effectively. Social proof (reviews, popularity) works as long as it looks authentic, and turns against the store as soon as it seems fabricated or exaggerated. Scarcity or urgency mentions speed up a decision if they're occasional and real, but become counterproductive if they're visibly permanent — a visitor used to e-commerce quickly identifies them as artificial.

## Part 19 — Frequently asked questions

Do you need a big budget to start? No — not having stock to finance reduces the starting stake needed; the realistic budget depends mostly on the ad test budget you can afford to lose. How long before you know whether a product works? There's no universal timeframe: what matters is enough data volume, not a fixed duration. Do you need a company from the start? No, the micro-enterprise is enough to start, but a business must be declared from the first euro of profit. How do you judge a reliable supplier? By comparing several candidates on their rating and order volume, then placing a test order before any volume commitment. Is dropshipping still viable with so much competition today? The market has changed, but the method — calculated margin, verified suppliers, careful customer service, prudent financial management — continues to separate the stores that last from those that close, regardless of the general level of competition.
