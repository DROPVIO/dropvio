# Part 34 — Frequently asked questions, continued

## What if two complementary products I thought were obvious don't sell well together?

Go back to the simple test mentioned in the part on complementary products: would a customer who bought the first immediately understand, without explanation, why the second is being offered to them? If the answer requires a complex justification despite a favorable initial intuition, the complementarity the seller perceives may not be the one the customer perceives — the questions asked of customer service often give a more reliable clue than the initial intuition.

## Is it serious not to have a cash reserve from the first month?

It's not a fault in itself, since a reserve is built gradually as the business generates a surplus. What matters is starting to build it as soon as possible, rather than putting this discipline off indefinitely on the pretext that the business is still young — it's precisely in the first months, the most uncertain, that this reserve is most sorely missed when something unexpected happens.

## How many backup suppliers should you qualify per product?

One is enough in most cases, as long as they've really been checked — test order included — and not simply identified on paper without ever being tested. Qualifying several backup suppliers for each product takes disproportionate time relative to the benefit, except for products that represent a very large share of revenue.

## Is a product that worked well for another seller still a good idea for me?

Rarely in the same way, as mentioned from the introduction of this course: a product that worked for someone else has often already been made public, which may have saturated part of its opportunity by the time you discover it. What deserves to be copied is never the product itself, but the method that let them find it and exploit it properly — that method, for its part, remains valid regardless of the saturation of a specific product.

## Should you answer every customer service request yourself, even once the business has grown?

Not necessarily in person for every message once a large volume is reached, but the quality and speed of the reply must stay constant regardless of who writes it — well-built reply templates, mentioned in this course, let you delegate part of this work without sacrificing quality, as long as you keep these templates customizable enough never to seem automatic in the face of real frustration.

# Part 35 — Mindset: what helps you last

Beyond the technical and financial aspects detailed in this course, a less often discussed dimension deserves to be named clearly: the ability to hold on over time in the face of results that never progress in a perfectly linear way.

## Accept that progress is never regular

A dropshipping business, like most commercial activities, doesn't progress regularly and predictably from one week to the next. Weeks of rapid progress alternate with weeks of stagnation, or even temporary decline, without necessarily meaning that the method applied is bad. Judging the health of the business on an isolated week rather than on a trend observed over several weeks, as already recommended for ad tests, applies just as much to your overall perception of the business's progress.

## Separate emotional attachment to a product from its objective evaluation

A seller who personally chose a product, sometimes after significant research time, often develops an attachment that makes it harder to accept objectively that the product isn't working despite a rigorous selection method followed at the start. Knowing how to recognize, from real data and not an impression, that a product should be dropped in favor of another, without seeing it as a personal failure, is one of the least technical but most decisive skills in this business.

## Keep track of what worked, not only what failed

It's natural to remember failures and difficulties more easily than slow but real progress. Keeping a regular record, even a simple one, of the decisions that paid off — a well-chosen supplier, a product page that converts particularly well, a customer reply that avoided a dispute — helps maintain a balanced view of the business, rather than a view biased only by the most recent and emotionally visible difficulties.
