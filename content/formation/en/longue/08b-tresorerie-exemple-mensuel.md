# Part 8 (supplement) — Reading a month of cash flow through a worked example

Part 8 laid out the principles. Here's how they translate into a concrete month of activity, with example amounts meant to illustrate the reasoning rather than serve as a universal benchmark.

## The month that looks excellent on revenue alone

A store collects 8,200 euros in revenue over a month. Seen in isolation, this figure looks encouraging, up on the previous month. Yet it's precisely at this moment that the most common mistake happens: assuming that this growth in revenue automatically translates into equivalent available cash to reinvest immediately.

## What this revenue becomes once the real items are deducted

Out of these 8,200 euros, product cost, import taxes on parcels and transaction fees represent, according to the margins calculated in Part 3 for the products sold that month, about 3,400 euros. The ad budget spent over the month comes to 2,300 euros. Refunds processed this month, including those linked to the previous month's orders that come back with a natural lag, represent 410 euros. The subscriptions and tools used to run the store — e-commerce platform, management tools, any automation or analytics subscriptions — represent 140 euros. What remains, before contributions, is about 1,950 euros.

## The share the safety reserve must absorb before any reinvestment

Out of these 1,950 euros, the URSSAF contributions to set aside on this collected revenue represent about 1,009 euros, as detailed in the calculation in Part 8, to put aside immediately rather than discover at declaration time. That leaves about 941 euros of margin really available for this particular month. It's only from this amount, and not from the initial 8,200 euros, that the question arises of what can be reinvested in advertising, put in reserve, or withdrawn to pay for the time spent on the business.

## Why the next month can behave very differently

The next month, a supplier lengthens its delivery time on a specific product, which mechanically pushes the refund rate on that product up from 8% to 15% within a few weeks — an example of the kind of signal mentioned in Part 8, which eats into the apparent margin without gross revenue showing it immediately. A safety reserve built up over previous months lets you absorb this shock without calling the business's overall cash position into question, while you identify the problem and respond to it, for example by reassessing that supplier with the grid in Part 5.

## What this example shows beyond the precise figures

The amounts used here are a teaching example, not a standard to reproduce — every store has its own cost structure. What this example does show, however, is universal: the figure that should guide management decisions is never the gross revenue displayed first on a dashboard, but what really remains after deducting, in order, product costs, transaction fees, advertising, refunds, tools, and the contributions to set aside. It's this final figure, often well below the initial intuition, that should guide reinvestment decisions.
