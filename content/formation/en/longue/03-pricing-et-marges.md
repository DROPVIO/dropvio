# Part 3 — Setting your prices and calculating your real margin

## Revenue isn't your friend, net profit is

This is probably the most expensive confusion among beginners: looking at the revenue a store generates and concluding that the business works. A store doing 10,000 euros in sales in a month may be losing money if the costs around each sale aren't counted properly. Revenue tells you people are buying. Only net profit, calculated after subtracting absolutely every cost, tells you whether you have a viable business.

The classic trap is to include only the product cost at the supplier in your calculation, forgetting everything else. Yet that "everything else" often represents a bigger share than the product cost itself.

## All the costs to include, in the order people forget them

The product cost at the supplier is the most visible, the one everyone naturally includes. Next comes the transaction cost: payment processors take a percentage of each sale, usually around 1.5 to 3% depending on the processor and the card type, plus a fixed amount per transaction. On a low-priced product, that fixed amount weighs proportionally much more heavily than on an expensive product — a point many overlook by thinking only in percentages.

Advertising acquisition cost is the third item, and often the most underestimated at the start. It's not just the amount spent on advertising divided by the number of sales: it's that amount attributed specifically to this product, which means tracking your campaigns with enough granularity to know what acquiring a customer really costs on this exact product, rather than a store-wide average that hides huge gaps between a product that converts well and one that doesn't convert at all.

The return and refund rate is the fourth item, the one beginners often discover after the fact, once the first returns start coming in. Depending on the product category, this rate can represent a significant share of sales — higher on clothing where size and fit cause problems, lower on simple accessories. Ignoring this item when setting your price means discovering your real margin only after spending your ad budget, which is the worst possible moment to find out.

Applicable taxes, starting with VAT for a business in France, must be built into the selling price calculation from the start and not added afterwards. We cover this point in depth in the part on the legal framework, but remember now that VAT isn't a cost you can "forget" thinking you'll settle it later with no impact on your margin — it's a levy on what you collect that must be anticipated in your selling price.

Import duties and taxes form a separate item, which many discover only with their first blocked parcel. Since July 1, 2026, the European Union applies a flat tax of 3 euros per item category on parcels under 150 euros shipped from a non-EU country — exactly the case of a typical AliExpress order delivered in France. On a 15-euro product, those 3 euros alone represent 20% of the selling price: it's the item that has most changed the equation for low-priced loss-leader products. Outside the EU, each country has its own thresholds and customs duties, to check for each destination.

Finally, the cost of the platform itself — your e-commerce tool subscription, any additional app fees — must be spread over all your monthly sales to know its real impact per product, even though it's fixed and not tied to each individual transaction.

## Why the calculation changes radically between France and international sales

A seller operating only as a micro-entrepreneur in France doesn't have the same cost structure as a seller invoicing customers in several countries with different VAT rules. In France, the micro-entrepreneur regime applies social contributions (URSSAF) calculated on revenue collected, around 12.3% for a goods-selling activity — a percentage that adds to your other costs and must be in your calculation from the start, not as a surprise at year end.

Internationally, the logic changes: depending on destination countries, intra-EU VAT thresholds and customs rules vary, which complicates the calculation but remains unavoidable if you sell beyond your national borders. ## Build a price grid, not an isolated price

Setting prices product by product, by instinct, doesn't build a coherent pricing strategy. A price grid thinks in tiers: an accessible entry point that catches attention and reassures on value for money, a mid-range offer that remains your main lead product, and a premium or bundle offer that raises average order value without extra acquisition effort — since the customer is already buying, offering a complementary product costs much less than going to find a new customer for an equivalent extra sale. We detail this complementary-product logic in the next part, devoted to the store itself.

## The "round" price mistake and the "slashed" price mistake

Two opposite reflexes cause problems. The first is setting a round price for convenience (20 euros rather than 19.90) thinking it simplifies reading — in reality, research on buying behavior consistently shows that a price just under a psychological threshold (19.90 rather than 20, 49 rather than 50) improves perceived value without significantly changing the margin. The second is the opposite excess: slashing your price aggressively to look competitive, without checking that the resulting margin still covers all the costs detailed above. A slashed price that no longer covers your real costs isn't a commercial strategy, it's an organized loss on every sale.

## Use a margin calculation before launching, not after

The simplest rule to remember from this whole part: never launch an ad campaign on a product before calculating, with all the cost items listed above, the exact net margin that product can generate at your planned selling price.
