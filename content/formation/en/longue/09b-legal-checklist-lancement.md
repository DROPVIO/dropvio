# Part 9 (supplement) — Administrative checklist before the first public launch

*This checklist repeats the general guidelines of Part 9. As stated in that part, it does not replace checking your specific situation with an accountant or official resources.*

## Before even creating the store

Check the legal status best suited to your starting situation — in the vast majority of cases at launch, the micro-enterprise, knowing from the start its revenue thresholds and when you'll need to change. Start the business registration process early enough, taking into account the administrative delay for obtaining the SIRET number mentioned in Part 9, which can't be compressed. Look into, from this stage, activating a payment collection solution, whose delay can also take several weeks and which must never be started at the last minute before a planned launch.

## Before putting the store online

Write, or have written, terms and conditions of sale that match your actual business, not a generic template copied without adaptation. Put in place a privacy policy compliant with the European data protection regulation, especially if third-party tools (advertising, audience analytics) collect visitor data. Clearly display the legal notice identifying who operates the site. Explicitly state the terms of the right of withdrawal and return, consistent with the policy actually applied internally — a gap between what's written and what's really applied when a return is requested is a frequent source of avoidable disputes, as seen in Part 7.

## On VAT and the taxation applicable to sales

Check, with a professional if in doubt, whether the VAT exemption still applies at your forecast or already reached revenue level. If sales are made to customers in other European countries, look into the IOSS scheme mentioned in Part 9 for low-value shipments, which simplifies VAT collection and avoids bad customs surprises for the customer, and build into your prices the European tax of 3 euros per item category on parcels under 150 euros coming from outside the EU. Never assume that a rule learned at a given moment stays valid indefinitely — this framework changes, as stressed in Part 9.

## On an ongoing basis, once the business is launched

Set aside social contributions as soon as money comes in rather than as the deadline approaches, as detailed in Part 8. Review your tax and social situation as soon as a significant revenue threshold is reached or approached, anticipating rather than reacting after the fact. Keep an organized record of supplier invoices and proof of payment received, useful both for day-to-day management and for any possible audit.

## The principle running through this whole checklist

Each of these points, taken in isolation, can seem like a secondary formality next to the urgency of launching the store and generating the first sales. It's precisely this inverted hierarchy of priorities that explains why so many sellers find themselves, several months after a commercially successful launch, having to urgently regularize an administrative situation they could have handled calmly from the start, when it required the least effort.
