# Part 6 (supplement) — Building and reading an acquisition budget over the first weeks

Part 6 laid out the principles of acquisition. Here's how to concretely structure a test budget over the first weeks of running a new product, and how to read the results without drawing premature conclusions.

## Why test before committing a significant budget

The riskiest reflex for a seller who has just chosen a promising product is to immediately commit a large ad budget, carried by launch enthusiasm. A deliberately limited test budget over the first days lets you check hypotheses before validating them at larger scale — does the real acquisition cost come close to the estimate used in the margin calculation in Part 3, or is it far enough off to call into question the product's viability at this selling price?

## Split the test budget into tiers, not a single block

A more prudent approach than a single budget poured in all at once is to split the test into two or three successive tiers, each funded enough to produce a meaningful amount of data, but each also reassessed before moving on to the next. A first tier is used to rule out creative approaches or targeting that are clearly ineffective. A second tier concentrates the budget on what showed a positive signal in the first tier, with enough data volume to confirm that signal rather than assume it on a number of results still too small to be reliable.

## What an isolated figure never says

An acquisition cost observed on only a handful of conversions remains statistically fragile, whatever the figure — a very good result on little data has a good chance of deteriorating once volume increases, just as a bad result on little data can improve with more volume. The temptation to cut a campaign after just a few hours on a disappointing result, or on the contrary to bet everything on it after an exceptional first result, is one of the most common mistakes among sellers discovering paid acquisition — in both cases, the decision is made on a sample too small to be really interpretable.

## Tie every decision to the margin calculation, never to revenue alone

An acquisition cost is never judged in absolute terms, but always relative to the available margin calculated in Part 3 for that specific product. An acquisition cost of nine euros can be excellent on a product whose margin before acquisition exceeds twenty euros, and catastrophic on a product whose margin before acquisition tops out at ten euros. That's why the margin calculation must always precede the launch of a campaign, as recalled in the checklist in Part 3 — without that benchmark, no acquisition figure can be correctly interpreted, however precise it is.

## Stop depending on a single channel as soon as possible

An acquisition budget concentrated on a single channel exposes the entire business to that channel's changes — an algorithm change, a general rise in costs, an ad account suspension for a sometimes vague reason. Gradually diversifying acquisition channels, once a first channel has proven viable, reduces this dependence without prematurely scattering a still-limited budget across untested channels — diversification comes after validation, not before.

## Summary grid for an acquisition test

Product net margin recalculated and confirmed before any ad launch. Test budget split into tiers rather than poured in as a single block. Decision to cut or increase a tier made on sufficient data volume, never on the very first hours. Acquisition cost always related to the margin available for that specific product, never interpreted alone. Channel diversification considered once a first channel is validated, not before.
