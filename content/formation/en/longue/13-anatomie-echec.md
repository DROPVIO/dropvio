# Part 13 — Anatomy of a failure: what stores that close have in common

This course has talked a lot about what works. It's just as useful to look in detail at what doesn't work, through a composite case built from the failure patterns most frequently described in public accounts from dropshipping sellers — once again a teaching example, not the story of a specific real store.

## The enthusiastic launch, without prior calculation

The store chooses a product seen on social media, without checking its real trend over time or comparing several suppliers — the first supplier found seems to have a good price, which is enough to settle the decision. No complete margin calculation is done before launch: the seller just compares the selling price with the purchase price, without including transaction fees, real acquisition cost, or an assumed return rate. That's exactly the opposite of the method described in Parts 2 and 3 of this course.

## The ad budget poured in all at once

Carried by launch enthusiasm, the seller immediately commits a large ad budget, without splitting it into tiers. The first results, on a data volume still too low to be interpretable, look excellent — a few quick sales at a very low acquisition cost. The budget is then multiplied without waiting for confirmation on a larger volume, exactly the opposite of the tiered approach described in Part 6. The acquisition cost then rises sharply once volume increases, without the seller having anticipated this natural statistical normalization.

## No reserve, revealed at the first unforeseen event

All the revenue collected in the first weeks is pumped back into advertising, with no safety reserve built up. When the supplier, whose reliability had never been seriously checked, has a prolonged stock problem, the store has no financial room to absorb the slowdown while finding a solution — exactly the scenario the safety reserve described in Part 8 is meant to prevent.

## Customer service treated as a secondary constraint

The first customer complaints, linked to the delivery delay caused by the supplier problem, go unanswered for several days, for lack of time devoted to this area, neglected until then in favor of acquisition. Several customers, getting no reply, turn directly to a bank dispute rather than a standard refund request — exactly the shift described in Part 7 as the most costly consequence of customer service that's too slow.

## The payment provider's sanction

The store's dispute rate exceeds the payment provider's tolerance threshold within a few weeks. The account is suspended pending review, immediately cutting off any ability to collect new payments — a total halt of the business, whatever the quality of the rest of the store at that moment.

## What this composite case shows

None of these steps alone is an immediate catastrophe. It's their chain, each one making the next worse, that turns an enthusiastic launch into a closure within a few months. And it's precisely this chain that each discipline detailed in this course — margin calculation, supplier comparison, tiered budgets, safety reserve, responsive customer service — is meant to break at the earliest possible link, before the following links kick in.

## The difference between caution and paralysis

It would, however, be excessive to conclude from this case that you need to move with a caution that prevents any action. The method described in this course isn't meant to delay a launch indefinitely while seeking a certainty that will never exist in a commercial activity — it's meant to replace improvisation with quick but systematic checks, which take a few hours or a few days, not several months. A seller who applies this method launches products almost as quickly as one who improvises, with a much better chance of lasting.
