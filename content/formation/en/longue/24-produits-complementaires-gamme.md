# Part 24 — Building a range of complementary products that sells itself

The choice of complementary products has been mentioned several times in this course — in Part 2 for range coherence, in Part 3 for its numerical effect on margin, in Part 4 for cross-selling on the product page. This part is devoted entirely to it, because it's one of the most profitable levers and yet one of the least methodically built by beginner sellers, who often add products to their catalog through one-off finds rather than a thought-out range logic.

## Three types of complementarity, not to be confused

A product can complement another in three different ways, which don't have the same commercial effect. Functional complementarity concerns a product needed to use the first — an accessory that extends or facilitates use of the main product. Usage complementarity concerns products used in the same context or moment, without direct functional dependence between them — two products meeting the same moment of the day or the same activity. Upgrade complementarity concerns a higher or more complete version of the same product, offered after a first, more modest purchase. These three types aren't built the same way in a product page or a cross-selling strategy, and mistaking one for another often produces an offer the customer sees as irrelevant.

## Why functional complementarity converts best in immediate cross-selling

A product whose usefulness depends directly on the main product — for example, a consumable or an accessory needed for normal use of the purchased product — is naturally offered at the time of purchase, because the customer immediately understands why it's being offered. It's this type of complementarity that was used in the worked example in Part 3 to illustrate the effect on margin: acquisition cost stays the same, but the cart value increases significantly, without much extra persuasion effort since the relevance of the addition is obvious to the customer.

## How to spot these complementarities from your own data

Rather than guessing in advance which complementary products will work, real sales data, once a first product is launched, often points to concrete leads: the questions most frequently asked of customer service about using the main product sometimes reveal an unmet need that a specific complementary product would satisfy, exactly the kind of signal Part 7 encourages you to follow rather than ignore once handled case by case.

## Avoid the trap of a range that grows without coherence

The opposite mistake, just as common, is adding products to the catalog simply because they're individually promising according to the criteria in Part 2, without checking their coherence with the rest of the range. A store accumulating unrelated products loses the positioning readability mentioned in Part 22 on the choice between niche and general, and complicates cross-selling since no natural complementary offer emerges from a disparate catalog.

## Build a range gradually, in concentric circles

An approach that works for many stores is to start from a solid main product, validated using the method in Part 2, then add products in concentric circles of decreasing complementarity: first direct functional complements, then products with the same use, and finally any upgrades once a base of loyal customers already exists to welcome them, in connection with the retention detailed in Part 17. This gradual construction, rather than a catalog widened all at once at launch, lets you check at each step that the addition really strengthens the range rather than diluting it.

## The final test before adding a product to the range

Before each addition, one simple question sums up this whole part: would a customer who has already bought the main product immediately understand, without further explanation, why this new product is being offered to them at this exact moment? If the answer requires a complex justification, the product probably isn't complementary in the sense that matters commercially, whatever its intrinsic quality otherwise.
