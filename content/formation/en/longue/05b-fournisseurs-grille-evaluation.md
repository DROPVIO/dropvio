# Part 5 (supplement) — Supplier evaluation grid before a first commitment

Part 5 explained why supplier reliability weighs as much as the product itself. Here's a concrete grid to apply before choosing a supplier for a launch, rather than relying on a quick impression of the product listing.

## Publicly available signals, and their honest limits

The product's average rating and the volume of orders already placed are the two most accessible public signals, and they're the ones to compare first between several potential suppliers for the same product. You must remain honest, however, about what these signals do and don't say: a high rating on a still-low order volume is statistically less reliable than a slightly lower rating on a much higher volume, because a small number of reviews can be abnormally positive or negative by simple sampling chance. A complete history of the supplier store's seniority, or an exhaustive record of its past disputes, remains out of reach of the public tools available to an independent seller — a real limit that's better to know and build into your caution than to pretend you don't have.

## The test order: the only way to check what no data shows

No public figure replaces direct experience. Placing a small test order before any volume launch lets you check three things no average rating guarantees precisely for your situation: the real shipping time from this specific supplier to your target delivery area, the real quality of the product received compared with the photos and description, and the quality of the packaging on arrival, an often overlooked point that nonetheless directly affects the end customer's first impression. This step costs a little time and a little money before even the first real customer, but it costs infinitely less than a volume launch with a supplier who turns out to be disappointing afterwards, once dozens of orders are already committed.

## Communication: an underestimated signal

How quickly and clearly a supplier answers questions asked before committing — about delivery times, possible packaging customization, handling a potential stock problem — is an often neglected but highly predictive signal of how that same supplier will behave once a real problem arises after launch. A supplier who is hard to reach or evasive even before the first order is unlikely to suddenly become responsive once a real problem comes up and the pressure rises.

## Never depend on a single supplier for a star product

A principle already mentioned in Part 5 deserves to be repeated here as a practical rule: for any product that becomes significant in revenue, identifying and qualifying at least one backup supplier, even without actively using them, considerably reduces the risk that a stock-out or sudden quality decline at the main supplier shuts down a large part of the business overnight. It's a precaution that costs only the qualification time, and can make the difference between an incident handled in a few hours and an interruption of several weeks.

## Summary grid before a first commitment

Average rating and order volume compared across several candidate suppliers rather than accepted in isolation. Test order placed and received before any volume launch. Real product and packaging quality checked on receipt. Responsiveness and clarity of communication tested before committing, not only after a problem. Second supplier identified in reserve for any product likely to become significant. Decision to choose this supplier documented with the elements that justified it, so it can be reassessed objectively if quality later declines.
