# Part 15 — Understanding why a visitor buys, or doesn't

Part 4 detailed the concrete elements of an effective product page. This part steps back a little to explain the psychological mechanisms that make those elements effective, drawing on principles widely documented in the literature on online consumer behavior — notably the public work of the Baymard Institute on cart abandonment and the usability principles popularized by the Nielsen Norman Group.

## Perceived friction weighs more than real friction

One of the most consistent findings in this literature is that what drives a visitor away isn't necessarily the real difficulty of a buying process, but the difficulty perceived when they assess it. A checkout form that's objectively quick, but seems long because it reveals its fields progressively without saying how many steps remain, can discourage more than a longer form whose every step is clearly announced. This distinction explains why elements like a progress bar at checkout, or a number of steps announced in advance, reduce abandonment without actually shortening the process.

## Uncertainty costs more than the price itself

A visitor rarely hesitates only over the displayed price — they hesitate over all the unknowns surrounding the purchase: the reliability of a seller they don't know, whether the product really matches what's shown, how easy a return would be if the product doesn't suit. It's this accumulation of uncertainties, more than price alone, that explains why two stores selling an identical product at the same price can have very different conversion rates: the one that most effectively reduces these uncertainties, through the reassurance detailed in Part 4, converts better, regardless of price.

## Social proof works, but only if it looks authentic

Customer reviews and popularity indicators work because an uncertain visitor naturally looks to the judgment of other people in a similar situation. This mechanism reverses, however, as soon as the social proof looks fabricated or exaggerated — a number of reviews clearly out of proportion with the store's apparent size, or unanimously perfect reviews without the slightest nuance, trigger a distrust that cancels the intended effect, exactly as mentioned in the audit grid in Part 4.

## The role of scarcity and urgency, to handle with care

Mentions of limited stock or a time-limited offer can speed up a purchase decision by reducing the temptation to put it off. This lever, however, loses all its effectiveness, and even becomes counterproductive, if it's visibly permanent — a countdown that restarts endlessly on every visit, or "almost sold out" stock displayed continuously for months, are quickly identified as artificial by visitors used to e-commerce practices, which damages the credibility of the whole store rather than speeding up conversion.

## What this understanding concretely changes

Understanding these mechanisms isn't about manipulating a visitor against their interest, but about building a buying experience that honestly reduces real friction and uncertainty rather than ignoring it. A product page built with these mechanisms in mind isn't only more convincing in the short term; it also produces fewer returns and less post-purchase dissatisfaction, because it properly informed the customer before their decision rather than simply pushing them toward a click.
