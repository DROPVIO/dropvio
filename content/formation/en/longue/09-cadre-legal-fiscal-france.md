# Part 9 — Legal and tax framework in France

*Disclaimer: this part gives general guidelines for understanding the framework that applies to dropshipping in France. It does not replace the advice of an accountant or lawyer on your specific situation — thresholds, rates and obligations change regularly, and some choices (legal status in particular) depend on your personal situation. Before any binding decision, have these points confirmed by a professional.*

## Why this topic is often dealt with too late

Many sellers start their dropshipping business telling themselves they'll regularize their administrative situation once the business has proven profitable. It's an understandable but risky line of reasoning: an undeclared commercial activity remains illegal from the first euro of profit made, regardless of the amount, and the consequences of a late regularization are almost always heavier than those of declaring from the start. Dealing with this topic upfront, even simply, saves you from having to unwind months of undeclared activity the day the administration takes an interest.

## Choosing a status: the micro-enterprise as the most common starting point

To start a dropshipping business solo, the micro-enterprise status (formerly "auto-entrepreneur") remains the most frequent choice, for its simplicity of creation and lighter administration compared with a company. This status applies social contributions calculated directly on revenue collected, with no cash advance on contributions that would be due even without a sale — a real advantage for starting without disproportionate financial risk.

The micro-enterprise does, however, have limits you need to know before committing: an annual revenue ceiling beyond which the regime no longer applies, a VAT exemption that also disappears above a certain revenue threshold, then requiring you to charge VAT to your customers, and no possibility of deducting your real costs from your taxable income since the regime applies a flat-rate allowance rather than a detailed calculation of your costs. For a business that grows significantly, a company (EURL, SASU) can become more relevant, notably to deduct real costs often higher than the flat-rate allowance, but this move comes with heavier accounting that generally justifies hiring an accountant at that stage.

## VAT in dropshipping: a topic that has become more complex

VAT is probably the point most misunderstood by dropshipping sellers, partly because the rules have changed significantly in recent years. Historically, some sellers charged VAT only on their margin, treating the purchase from the supplier and the resale as two separate operations for tax purposes. Successive European reforms have tightened this framework, particularly around distance sales of imported goods: the general rule now is that VAT must be applied to the total price charged to the customer, not only to your margin, as soon as you sell to consumers.

The scheme called IOSS (Import One-Stop Shop) simplifies declaring and paying this VAT for shipments worth no more than 150 euros, by letting you collect VAT at the time of sale rather than letting the customer discover it at customs — a difference that matters enormously for the customer experience, since a parcel stuck in customs with surprise fees to pay is one of the surest ways to turn a satisfied customer into a furious one. Above this 150-euro threshold, customs rules become more complex and deserve specific guidance. An important change has applied since July 1, 2026: parcels under 150 euros shipped from a non-EU country bear a European flat tax of 3 euros per item category. It comes on top of VAT and weighs directly on the margin, especially on low-priced products — so it must be included in the calculation from Part 3.

## The information obligation that protects as much as it constrains

Beyond taxation, a French online store is subject to obligations to inform its customers that exist to protect the consumer, but that also protect you by clarifying the rules of the game from the start: clearly accessible terms and conditions of sale, a privacy policy compliant with the European data protection regulation (GDPR), clear information on the right of withdrawal and return terms, and a legal notice identifying who operates the site. These documents aren't mere decorative formalities — in a dispute with a customer, the absence or vagueness of this information systematically works against you, while a clear, well-written policy is on the contrary a sign of seriousness that reassures the customer even before a problem arises, as we saw in the part on the store that converts.

## The administrative delay no one anticipates

A practical point deserves mention, because it regularly surprises new sellers: obtaining a SIRET number and fully activating an account to collect online payments through a solution like Stripe take administrative time, sometimes several weeks depending on circumstances. This delay can't be compressed by the seller's willpower — it depends on the processing times of the bodies concerned. Starting these procedures several weeks before the planned launch date, rather than trying to trigger everything at the same time as launching the store itself, saves you from ending up with a store that's ready but unable to legally collect a single payment.

## A final warning on how fast this framework changes

What makes this topic particularly tricky is that the tax rules applicable to e-commerce and distance selling keep changing, in France as at European level. What was true two years ago may have changed today. Good practice is therefore not to memorize a fixed rule from this course, but to get into the habit of regularly checking your situation with an accountant or official resources (service-public.fr, the French tax website, URSSAF), especially when your business crosses a revenue threshold that could change the regime that applies to you.
