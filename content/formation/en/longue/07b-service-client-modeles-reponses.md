# Part 7 (supplement) — Four reply templates to adapt, never to copy as is

Part 7 explained why preparing template replies changes the quality and speed of customer service. Here are four outlines for the most common situations, to be systematically customized with your store's tone and the real details of each order — a reply visibly copied word for word has the opposite effect of the one intended.

## Delivery delay flagged proactively, before the customer complains

The outline always starts by acknowledging the fact before justifying it: say that the parcel has taken longer than expected, give an updated estimate as precise as possible rather than a vague phrase, and offer a concrete gesture if the delay exceeds a threshold you'll have set in advance for your store — a small credit, a discount on the next order, or simply an explicit guarantee of an automatic refund if the parcel doesn't arrive within an additional set time. The most important element of this outline isn't the goodwill gesture, it's contacting the customer before they worry and write themselves, as stressed in Part 7.

## Product damaged or not as described on arrival

This outline must first clearly acknowledge the problem without minimizing or trying to justify it, then ask for a photo if one hasn't already been provided — a request that must be phrased as a help toward a quick resolution, never as doubting the customer's word. It then offers an explicit choice between sending the product back or a refund, rather than imposing a single option, and states a clear processing time once the customer's choice is known.

## Size or color mismatch with the customer's expectation

This situation calls for particular nuance: you need to distinguish a real shipping error from a misunderstanding of the product page itself, without ever turning that distinction into a reproach to the customer. The outline acknowledges the inconvenience, offers an exchange or a return depending on what's feasible given the product's value and the cost of a physical return, and — if the problem comes from an ambiguous product page — this information must be passed on internally to correct the page, exactly as the audit grid in Part 4 suggests: a repeated customer report on the same point is a direct signal that the page needs reworking rather than continuing to treat the symptom case by case.

## Refund request, including outside the most favorable grounds

Faced with a refund request that doesn't exactly match a non-conformity ground, the outline avoids the defensive reflex of immediately listing the conditions of the return policy. It starts by understanding the real reason for the dissatisfaction, offers a solution before mentioning any limits, and implicitly reminds — never as a direct threat — that a smooth experience is precisely what keeps the customer from turning to a bank dispute, the most costly situation mentioned in Part 7, for you as well as for them.

## The principle common to these four outlines

In each of these situations, the structure stays the same: acknowledge before justifying, offer a concrete solution before the customer has to demand it, and set a clear timeline rather than a vague promise. It's this structure, more than the exact wording of each outline, that must stay constant from one reply to the next — the wording itself must always sound as if it had just been written for that specific customer.
