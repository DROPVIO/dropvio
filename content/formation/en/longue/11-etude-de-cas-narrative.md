# Part 11 — A year in the life of a store, from launch to stability

The previous ten parts dealt with each topic separately. This part brings them together in a continuous story — a fictional store, followed month by month over its first year, to show how these principles really fit together over time rather than in the artificially tidy order of a table of contents. The figures and events are constructed for teaching purposes; this isn't the story of a real store, but a plausible composite of the most frequently encountered situations.

## Month 1 — The cautious launch

The store starts with a single product, chosen using the method in Part 2: modest but growing search volume, an underlying trend rather than a fad spike, a supplier chosen after comparing three candidates using the reliability criteria detailed in Part 5. The margin calculation in Part 3 was done before launch, with a deliberately prudent acquisition cost assumption. The first month's ad budget is limited, split into two tiers as recommended in Part 6. The first sales arrive, at a modest but steady pace — the goal of this month wasn't volume, but validating the model on real data rather than assumptions.

## Month 2 — The first warning signal, dealt with early

A supplier announces a longer than usual shipping time. Rather than waiting for customers to start complaining, the store proactively contacts those whose orders are affected, following the logic of Part 7: acknowledge the delay before the customer discovers it themselves. In parallel, a second supplier is identified in reserve for this product, as recommended in the grid in Part 5 — a precaution that won't be used immediately, but reduces the risk if the first supplier keeps declining.

## Months 3 to 4 — Widening the range

Building on the first sales data, the store adds two complementary products to the first, rather than multiplying unrelated categories — the range-coherence logic mentioned in Part 2 and Part 4. A bundle offer is tested, pairing the main product with one of the complements, following exactly the numerical reasoning of example 3 in Part 3: the acquisition cost doesn't change for a customer who buys both products together, which mechanically improves the margin per customer with no extra acquisition effort.

## Month 5 — The first real blow

A product representing about a quarter of revenue goes through a prolonged stock-out at the main supplier. That's when the second supplier identified in month 2 becomes useful: the transition takes a few days rather than several weeks of searching in a hurry. The safety reserve built up since the first month, in line with Part 8, absorbs the slowdown in revenue over this period without straining overall cash flow.

## Month 6 — Administrative regularization comes due

Cumulative revenue approaches a threshold that warrants checking the applicable tax regime, in line with the checklist in Part 9. A quick consultation with an accountant confirms that the micro-enterprise remains suitable for now, but identifies the future point when it will no longer be if growth continues at this pace — an anticipation that avoids a late, rushed discovery.

## Months 7 to 9 — Gradual ramp-up and its adjustments

The ad budget increases gradually, never abruptly, with constant tracking of acquisition cost relative to each product's margin as detailed in Part 6. A slight erosion of the conversion rate on the main product's page is spotted through a regular audit using the grid in Part 4 — the page is reworked, notably on reassurance placed too low on the page, and the conversion rate recovers in the following weeks.

## Month 10 — A dispute, treated as a signal rather than an isolated accident

A first bank dispute occurs on an order. Rather than treating it as an isolated incident with no follow-up, the store notices, through its regular tracking of indicators mentioned in Part 7, that this dispute is part of a slight rise in the refund rate on a specific product over several weeks — exactly the kind of cumulative signal described in Part 10. The cause is identified: an imprecise description on a compatibility point, corrected immediately on the page.

## Months 11 to 12 — Consolidate before accelerating further

Rather than continuing to accelerate unchecked on the momentum of recent months, the end of the year is devoted to consolidating what works: documenting the product choices that succeeded, as recommended in Part 2, revising the safety reserve upward to cover a now higher level of activity, and first thoughts about a stronger brand identity, in the spirit of the evolution described in Part 10 — not an abrupt change of model, but a first step in that direction.

## What this story shows beyond the specific case

None of these situations is exceptional on its own — a stock-out, a dispute, a product page adjustment are everyday events in the life of any online store. What distinguishes this fictional store from one that would have closed within the year is therefore not the absence of problems, but how each problem was anticipated through upfront preparation, or handled quickly thanks to regular tracking rather than discovered late. That's exactly the central thesis of this course, from start to finish: the difference between stores that last and those that give up has less to do with luck than with method.
