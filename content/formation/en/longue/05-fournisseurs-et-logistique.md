# Part 5 — Suppliers and logistics: staying reliable

## Why this topic deserves a whole part

We saw it in Part 1: delivery times and supplier reliability are among the friction points most often raised by dropshipping sellers, well ahead of most other difficulties. That's no accident. Unlike a traditional business where you control your stock and shipping, dropshipping makes you dependent on a link you don't directly control — the supplier — while leaving you fully responsible toward the end customer. This asymmetry between control and responsibility is the source of most of the problems we'll deal with here.

## What can go wrong with a supplier, and why you don't see it coming

A supplier can change a product's quality without warning, quietly switching material or subcontractor to cut their own costs. They can run out of stock without immediately updating their listing, which makes you sell a product you actually can't deliver. They can also simply stop answering, or close their store, leaving your pending orders with no solution. None of these scenarios is visible in advance in a product's raw numbers — only an assessment of the seller's profile gives an indication of the risk.

## The signals to check before committing to a supplier

The product rating and number of reviews are the first filter, the most accessible one. A product with a high rating over a large number of reviews is a much stronger trust signal than an equally high rating over only a few reviews, where a perfect score may simply reflect a sample too small to be meaningful.

The volume of orders already handled by that specific seller, beyond the product itself, gives an indication of their operational seriousness — a seller who has handled substantial volume for a long time has probably refined their shipping and stock-management processes, while a recent seller with little history remains an unknown, not necessarily bad, but to be treated with more caution.

When the same product or a very close equivalent is available from several sellers, which happens very often on AliExpress, comparing these profiles before choosing rather than after building your store around a single seller saves you from being trapped the day that seller disappoints. That's precisely why you should, from the start, identify other sellers offering an equivalent on AliExpress yourself and rank them by product rating and order volume — so you have a fallback option already identified rather than having to search in a hurry the day your initial supplier causes problems.

## What this assessment can't tell you yet

You need to be honest about an important limit: the information available through product research tools, including what Dropvio surfaces, concerns the product's rating and volume, not a real reliability history of the store over time (exact seniority, the seller's own dispute rate). This finer data exists at AliExpress but isn't exposed by the search interfaces available to sellers like you. That doesn't make assessment by rating and volume useless, far from it, but it means it must remain a probability filter and not an absolute guarantee. For a product on which you plan to invest a substantial ad budget, ordering a sample yourself before launching at scale remains the most reliable check, despite the delay and small cost involved.

## Anticipating rather than suffering a delivery delay

Delivery time itself remains, for now, hard data to compare reliably between suppliers through the tools available to an independent seller — that precise information generally requires technical access reserved for direct commercial partnerships with sourcing platforms, rather than a consumer search tool. What you can control, however, is transparency toward your customer: announcing a realistic rather than optimistic delivery time, keeping the customer informed in case of delay rather than leaving them without news, and having a clear policy on what happens if the announced time is exceeded. A customer told in advance about a two-to-three-week delivery time doesn't feel deceived when the order arrives within that time. A customer promised three days who waits three weeks feels deceived, even if the final product is perfect.

## Diversify rather than depend on a single link

The most general lesson of this part goes beyond choosing a supplier: the more your business depends on a single outside link you don't control — a single supplier, a single ad platform, a single sales channel — the more fragile it is to an incident on that specific link. Identifying a reliable alternative in advance for each important product in your store, before you even need it, is a simple discipline that saves you precious time the day an incident really happens, rather than leaving you searching for a solution in a hurry while orders pile up without an answer.
