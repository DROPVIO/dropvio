# Part 10 (supplement) — Table of signals to watch before they become problems

Part 10 stressed the importance of seeing things coming rather than suffering them. Here, topic by topic, are the concrete signals to monitor regularly, directly echoing each of the previous parts of this course.

## On the product and the supplier

A slight, gradual rise in the return rate on a specific product, even if each month it remains individually insignificant, deserves to be tracked over time rather than ignored until it crosses an arbitrary threshold — a rise of two points a month over three consecutive months tells a different story from an isolated one-off rise. A gradual lengthening of the delivery time announced by a supplier, even by a few days, can signal a broader decline to come rather than an isolated incident — it's the kind of signal that justifies restarting, without waiting, the comparison with alternative suppliers described in Part 5, before it worsens.

## On margin and finances

A net margin that slowly erodes over several weeks on a product, without any single item exploding spectacularly, is often the cumulative result of several small simultaneous declines — acquisition cost slightly up, return rate slightly up, miscellaneous costs slightly up — none of which, taken alone, would be enough to flag as urgent. That's why Part 8 insists on regularly tracking the real indicators rather than an occasional one-off check: only regular tracking lets you see this kind of cumulative decline before it becomes significant.

## On customer service and disputes

A customer service response time that gradually lengthens, generally because order volume has grown faster than the capacity to answer, almost always precedes a rise in the dispute rate detailed in Part 7 — the two developments are linked, the first often being the cause of the second with a lag of a few weeks. Monitoring the first signal lets you act before the second, more costly, materializes.

## On acquisition and channel dependence

Growing dependence on a single acquisition channel, which looks like a simple natural consequence of that channel working well, deserves to be actively monitored rather than seen as success with no downside — it's exactly the kind of risk concentration mentioned in Part 6, which exposes the entire business to any unfavorable change in that specific channel.

## The principle common to all these signals

In each of these cases, the early signal is almost always discreet, gradual, and easy to rationalize individually as an isolated incident with no consequences. It's the overview, tracked regularly rather than consulted only when in doubt, that turns these discreet signals into alerts you can act on in time — that's precisely the role played, together, by the various tracking tools built into Dropvio mentioned throughout this course: not replacing your vigilance, but giving it a reliable data base to work from.
