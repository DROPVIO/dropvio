# Part 2 — Finding and validating a winning product

## Why the hunt for a winning product is almost always done badly

If you ask a hundred people starting out in dropshipping how they choose their product, you'll hear roughly the same answer ninety times: they saw a viral video, they found the product "cool", and they jumped in. That's not a method, it's a bet based on an impression. The problem isn't that these people lack instinct — some have a real knack — the problem is that instinct without data to confirm or refute it produces a random result: sometimes it works, most of the time it doesn't, and above all you learn nothing from the failure because you never know why it didn't work.

A product selection method exists exactly for that: turning an intuition into a testable hypothesis. You never go in blind; you go in with numbers that tell you whether a product has real demand, a viable margin, and competition that won't crush you on day one.

## The four criteria that really matter

The volume of orders a product has already made is the first signal to look at, because it's proof of real demand rather than an assumption. A product with several hundred orders at a supplier has already convinced buyers; a product with zero orders is a complete bet on your ability to create demand yourself, which is much harder and more expensive in advertising than capturing demand that already exists.

Potential margin is the second criterion, and that's where most beginners go wrong by confusing an attractive selling price with a sufficient margin. A product at 8 euros that costs you 6 euros at the supplier leaves you almost nothing once advertising, transaction fees and returns are taken into account — we'll go through this calculation in depth in the next part, but remember now that a product only deserves to be tested if its gross margin, before any other cost, leaves real room to maneuver.

Saturation is the third criterion, the sneakiest because it isn't visible in a product's raw numbers. A product can have excellent order volume precisely because fifty different stores already sell it with the same image, the same supplier, the same promise. At that point, you're not entering a growing market, you're entering a price war against competitors who may have been optimizing their acquisition costs for months. The warning sign is a product whose image shows up identically on dozens of different stores after a quick search.

The fourth criterion, often forgotten, is the reliability of delivery times and of the supplier — a topic we develop in depth in Part 5, but which must already be part of your decision when choosing a product. A product excellent on the first three criteria, but shipped from a warehouse with a three-week lead time by a seller with almost no history, remains a risky bet: you can be right about the product and still lose because of execution.

## The order-volume "sweet spot"

There's a zone of order volume you can think of as the balance point: enough orders to prove real demand, not yet at the level that signals extreme saturation by dozens of well-established competitors. Conversely, a product with already very high volume (several thousand orders) isn't necessarily a bad choice, but you should assume competition is probably tough, and that your advantage will have to come from something other than simply selling this product — packaging, bundle offer, delivery speed, customer service, rather than the product alone.

## Checking that a product isn't already dead

A product may have had its moment of glory six months ago and be dying today — the fashion for an accessory or gadget often follows a fast curve rather than steady growth. Looking only at a snapshot of order volume at a given moment tells you nothing about direction: is this product breaking through, or running out of steam after being exploited by everyone?

That's why a history over time, even approximate, is worth more than an isolated figure. Dropvio keeps a daily snapshot of the order volume of tracked products, which lets you estimate a growth trend over recent days rather than relying on a single frozen capture — a product that's clearly progressing deserves more attention than a stable or declining one, even if its current raw volume is identical.

## Check the supplier before checking the product

An excellent product at a bad supplier remains a bad choice. Before even calculating your margin, look at the product rating, the number of reviews, and if possible how long the store has existed. A recent seller with very little history isn't automatically to be ruled out, but deserves more caution — maybe order a sample yourself before building an entire ad campaign around that product.

If the same product is available from several sellers, which is very common on AliExpress, comparing their profiles rather than taking the first link that comes along can save you from a supplier who changes quality without warning once you already have traction — search yourself for other sellers offering an equivalent, and compare them on product rating and order volume, to identify a reliable fallback option if your initial supplier disappoints.

## Build a shortlist rather than betting on a single product

A common mistake is to bet everything on a single "favorite" product, which amounts to playing a single card. The most robust method is to build a shortlist of three to five products that pass the four criteria, then test them with a modest ad budget each before doubling down on the one that responds best. It's neither slower nor more expensive than a single bet — it's simply a way of spreading risk over several hypotheses rather than one, exactly like any serious investor who refuses to put everything into a single asset.
