# Part 21 — Sources and bibliography

This part lists the categories of sources that informed the writing of this course, transparently, including their limits. The principle followed throughout this course has been to distinguish three levels of reliability: methodological frameworks published by recognized, documented organizations, official regulatory data, and figures widely quoted in the industry but whose primary origin cannot be verified with sufficient certainty — the latter are systematically presented in the text as approximations, never as established facts.

## On user experience and conversion (Part 4, Part 15)

The principles detailed on product pages, reassurance and purchase decision mechanisms draw on the methodological frameworks published publicly by the Nielsen Norman Group, a reference organization in interface usability research since the 1990s, and by the Baymard Institute, whose repeated studies on e-commerce cart abandonment are widely quoted in the online retail industry. Both organizations publish a significant part of their findings in open access, which allows direct verification of the principles mentioned in this course rather than a simple second-hand citation.

## On the French legal and tax framework (Part 9 and its supplement)

The regulatory elements cited — micro-enterprise status, VAT thresholds, the IOSS scheme, legal information obligations — draw on the framework published by the official resources of the French administration, notably service-public.fr, the French tax website, and URSSAF for the social contribution rates applicable to goods-selling activities. This framework changes regularly, as explicitly recalled in Part 9 itself: the rates and thresholds indicated must be checked with these same official resources or an accountant when you need them, rather than memorized for good from this reading.

## On general industry statistics (Part 1, and occasional mentions elsewhere)

The figures mentioned on the failure rate of dropshipping stores in their first year, on the proportion of sellers reaching a high level of revenue, or on the difficulties most frequently cited by sellers (delivery times, supplier reliability), come from content widely circulated in the e-commerce and dropshipping industry. A thorough check of their origin showed that these figures, although widely repeated from one source to another, trace back to specialized blogs and commercial platforms in the industry rather than to an academic or public statistical study with a clearly documented, independently verifiable methodology and sample. That's why these figures are systematically presented in this course as widely quoted orders of magnitude rather than confirmed data — a nuance that changes nothing in the lesson to draw from them, but avoids presenting you with a false statistical certainty.

## On the psychology of the purchase decision (Part 15)

Beyond the frameworks of the Nielsen Norman Group and the Baymard Institute already cited, the general principles on uncertainty, social proof and perceived scarcity draw on notions well established in consumer behavior psychology, popularized notably by the work of researchers such as Robert Cialdini on the mechanisms of influence — principles documented for several decades and applied here specifically to the context of e-commerce.

## A final note on the use of these sources

None of these sources has been used to justify a misleading commercial practice or manipulating the customer against their interest. On the contrary, they have served to honestly understand why certain practices work, with the aim of building a business that properly informs the customer rather than trying to get around their vigilance — a distinction that, beyond any ethical consideration, also produces more lasting commercial results, as detailed throughout this course.
