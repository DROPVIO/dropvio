# Part 25 — Anticipating seasonality rather than suffering it

Some products and some periods of the year behave predictably from one year to the next. This part details how to build this calendar dimension into the method already laid out in the previous parts, rather than discovering each seasonal peak or trough as it happens.

## Distinguish true seasonality from an underlying trend

Part 2 already mentioned the importance of observing a product over several months to distinguish an underlying trend from a one-off fad spike. Seasonality is a third, distinct category: a pattern that repeats every year at similar periods, predictably, without being either lasting growth or a passing fad. A strongly seasonal product can be an excellent commercial choice, provided you correctly anticipate the high and low periods rather than treating them as a surprise every cycle.

## Anticipate supplier delays before demand peaks

A supplier faced with a sudden rise in demand, as a well-known period of strong seasonality approaches, can see its shipping times lengthen significantly compared with a quieter period, simply because its own logistics capacity is being called on by many sellers at once. Anticipating orders and checking the announced delivery times several weeks before the expected peak, rather than at the peak itself, reduces the risk of a delivery time that deteriorates precisely when customer attention is highest.

## Adjust the acquisition budget to the calendar

Advertising acquisition cost itself varies across the year, notably upward during periods when many sellers compete for the attention of the same potential buyers. A margin calculation, as detailed in Part 3, must be revised as these periods approach rather than keeping an acquisition cost assumption established at a quieter time of the year — a product profitable in a normal period can become less attractive, or on the contrary stay very profitable if its demand grows faster than its acquisition cost, which can only be checked by recalculating rather than assuming.

## Prepare customer service in advance rather than in a rush

Periods of strong seasonality mechanically generate more customer service requests, especially around delivery times. Preparing in advance reply templates suited to this specific period, anticipating the most likely questions, saves you from improvising in a rush just when the volume of messages is highest — a direct extension of the logic detailed in Part 7 on reply templates prepared upfront.

## Use quiet periods to prepare rather than just wait

Periods of lower seasonal activity shouldn't be experienced as downtime, but as a window to prepare for the next period: researching new products using the method in Part 2, reviewing and enriching existing product pages using the grid in Part 4, or consolidating the cash position mentioned in Part 8 before the busier period that follows. A store that treats these quiet periods as a simple pause, without active preparation, often approaches the next period with a delay that a more forward-looking store doesn't have.
