# Part 6 — Acquisition and marketing

## Free traffic doesn't exist, it just changes currency

A common confusion among beginners is to set "paid advertising" against "free social media" as if one cost money and the other nothing at all. In reality, organic content on social media doesn't cost money directly, but it costs time, consistency and often several weeks before producing a visible result — a currency just as real as an ad budget, only less visible on a bank statement. Understanding this keeps you from underestimating the real investment a "free" strategy represents, and lets you choose knowingly rather than by default.

## Paid advertising: principle before tactics

The ad platforms most used in dropshipping remain Meta (Facebook and Instagram) and TikTok, with different mechanics but a common principle: you pay to show your product to people who weren't actively looking for it, unlike a Google search where the user already expresses purchase intent. This difference has a direct consequence for your message: on these platforms, you have to capture attention that wasn't looking for you, which requires a visual or video that stops the scroll in the first seconds, well before even talking about the product itself.

Precise tracking of what each euro spent brings in, product by product and campaign by campaign, is not optional: without it, you never know whether a campaign is profitable or losing you money with every click. This is one of the points where going back to the margin calculation detailed in Part 3 becomes concrete — knowing your net margin before launching a campaign tells you exactly how much you can afford to pay to acquire a customer on this specific product, without exceeding that threshold out of optimism or impatience.

## The risk of depending on a single platform

Ad platforms change their rules, their algorithms and sometimes close accounts without detailed prior warning, which can abruptly cut off all the traffic of a store that depended entirely on a single channel. This risk isn't theoretical: it regularly hits sellers who had built no alternative. The practical lesson isn't to avoid paid advertising, which remains one of the most effective ways to start quickly, but never to let that dependence become total — building in parallel an email list of customers, an organic presence even a modest one, or a second ad channel gives you a safety margin if the main channel stops one day.

## Organic content: slow to start, more stable over time

An organic presence on social media generally takes longer to pay off than an ad campaign, but it builds an asset that belongs more to you — an audience that follows you independently of any specific ad platform, and that costs less and less to reactivate once built. Consistency matters more than perfection here: posting modestly but consistently over several weeks gives better results than a single perfect post followed by silence.

## The role of the landing page in any acquisition strategy

Whatever channel brings the traffic, the page that traffic lands on remains where the final conversion happens — the direct link with the previous part on the store itself. An excellent ad campaign that sends people to a poorly converting page wastes much of its effectiveness before the customer has even been able to judge the product. Checking and fixing your sales page before launching a significant acquisition budget, rather than after, remains the most profitable move in this whole part.

## Content that sells without looking like it's selling

The content that works best on social media today rarely looks like a classic ad. It shows a real use of the product, solves a concrete problem the viewer recognizes in their own life, or tells an authentic story rather than a frontal sales pitch. The general public's distrust of obvious ads — a point already mentioned in Part 1 about the growing maturity of dropshipping customers — makes this more authentic content format clearly more effective than an old-school advertising approach, even with the same budget behind it.
