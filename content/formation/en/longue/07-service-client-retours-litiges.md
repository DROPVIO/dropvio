# Part 7 — Customer service, returns and disputes

## The moment your reputation is really decided

Most dropshipping sellers focus their attention on everything before the sale — finding the product, building the store, bringing in traffic — and relax that attention once the order is placed. That's a strategic mistake, because it's precisely after the sale that a store's lasting reputation is decided: a satisfied customer who had a problem handled well often becomes more loyal than a customer whose order simply went smoothly, because they were able to verify concretely that you take your responsibilities seriously.

## What the customer expects, even without saying it

A customer who contacts customer service rarely expects an instant solution to a complex problem — above all, they expect to be heard quickly and to feel that someone is really taking care of their situation. A long response time, even followed by a good solution, leaves a lasting negative impression, while a quick reply that acknowledges the problem and gives a clear timeline for resolution, even if the final solution takes time, is generally well received.

Faced with a delivery delay, proactive transparency — warning before the customer worries and complains — completely changes perception compared with a silent wait followed by a complaint. It's the direct extension of what was said in the part on suppliers: trust is built on communication, not on the absence of problems, since problems will happen anyway in a business that depends on an outside supplier.

## Structure your replies rather than improvising each time

Answering each complaint by improvising from scratch takes considerable time and produces replies of uneven quality. Preparing template replies in advance for the most common situations — delivery delay, product damaged on arrival, size or color mistake, refund request — lets you answer faster while keeping consistent quality, provided you keep these templates customizable enough never to come across as a cold, automatic reply to real frustration.

## Understanding the difference between a return, a refund and a dispute

These three situations only look alike on the surface and deserve to be clearly distinguished. A return is a customer request to send a product back, usually followed by a refund once the product is received, or in some cases without requiring a physical return given what it would cost to return a low-value item from abroad. A refund is giving the customer their money back, whether following a return, a goodwill gesture, or a mistake on your part. A dispute, on the other hand, is a situation where the customer contests the payment directly with their bank or payment method rather than going through you — the most serious of the three, because it escapes your direct control and is handled and judged by a third party.

## Why disputes deserve particular vigilance

Payment providers monitor a merchant account's dispute rate, and a rate above a certain threshold — around 1% of transactions depending on the provider — can trigger measures ranging from extra fees to outright suspension of the payment account. That's an existential risk for an online store: without a way to collect payments, the business stops immediately, whatever the quality of the rest of the store. A high dispute rate is almost never due to a single isolated incident; it's the cumulative symptom of several recurring problems not dealt with at the source — delivery times not kept, customer service too slow, products that don't match their description.

The best prevention against disputes remains, once again, transparency and responsiveness upstream: a customer who gets a quick, frictionless refund when their request is legitimate generally has no reason to file a dispute, which is also a slower and more constraining process for them than a direct refund.

## Track your incidents rather than discovering them one by one

A seller who handles each refund and each dispute as it comes, without an overview, never sees the trends taking shape — a particular product generating an abnormally high return rate, a period when disputes rise for no apparent reason, a supplier whose quality has visibly declined. That overview is what lets you act before the situation worsens, rather than reacting incident by incident without ever seeing the underlying problem.

Rather than manually rebuilding that view from dozens of scattered emails and notifications, centralize this tracking yourself — refunds and disputes, with an honest distinction between "no dispute recorded" and "data not available" — an important nuance, since full visibility on disputes depends on the payment method used, and a zero figure must never be confused with a real absence of possible tracking.

## The general rule that sums up this whole part

An after-sales problem handled well costs time but rarely money beyond the refund itself. An after-sales problem handled badly costs the customer, often several potential customers through a public negative review, and in the most serious cases, access to your very means of collecting payments. The cost-benefit ratio between handling after-sales service well and neglecting it is one of the most lopsided in the whole business — which makes it one of the areas where investing time pays off most, while it's precisely the one most sellers underinvest in.
