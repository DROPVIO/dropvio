# Part 23 — International shipping and customs: what to anticipate

A large part of dropshipping relies on shipping that crosses an international border between the supplier and the end customer. This part details the specific points of vigilance for this international dimension, as a complement to the tax framework detailed in Part 9.

## The parcel stuck in customs, one of the worst scenarios for customer satisfaction

A parcel held in customs, with additional fees for the customer to pay before delivery, is one of the most negative experiences a customer can have after a purchase — not only because of the extra delay, but above all because of the feeling of having been misinformed about the real cost of their purchase at checkout. The IOSS scheme, already mentioned in Part 9, directly addresses this problem for low-value shipments by allowing VAT to be collected at the time of sale rather than letting it show up as a customs surprise — a point worth checking with your shipping method and your main destination country. Since July 1, 2026, these same parcels under 150 euros coming from outside the EU also bear a European flat tax of 3 euros per item category, to be built into the price rather than left for the customer to discover. Outside the EU, each country applies its own thresholds: the United Kingdom, for example, charges no customs duty below 135 pounds.

## Delivery times vary by destination

A delivery time that seems reasonable for one destination isn't necessarily so for another, depending on local logistics infrastructure and each country's own customs procedures. Announcing a single delivery time valid for all destinations, without distinction, means systematically disappointing customers in the slowest destinations while being artificially cautious for the fastest — a realistic delivery range per major geographic zone, when sales volume justifies it, informs the customer more honestly than a single figure applied uniformly.

## Products subject to restrictions depending on the country

Some product categories, without being universally banned, are subject to restrictions or specific documents depending on the destination country — a point to check before offering a product internationally rather than discovering the restriction at a first customs block. This risk, generally limited to certain specific categories (electronic products with batteries, cosmetics, products that can be classed as medical devices), deserves an occasional check rather than general worry about the whole catalog.

## Packaging suited to a longer journey

A product shipped from the other side of the world goes through a longer and often rougher logistics journey than a local delivery, with more intermediate handling. Checking, during the test order mentioned in Part 5, that the packaging properly withstands this more demanding journey avoids a good share of complaints about products damaged on arrival, which weigh directly on the return rate built into the margin calculation in Part 3.

## What this international dimension changes in communicating with the customer

Clearly stating, right on the product page, that shipping is from abroad and that a longer delivery time than a local purchase should be expected defuses a good part of the impatience that would otherwise turn into a premature complaint. This transparency, in the spirit of the reassurance detailed in Part 4, costs a single line of text and avoids a frustration that would be discovered at delivery anyway — better for it to be anticipated at purchase than discovered as a bad surprise afterwards.
