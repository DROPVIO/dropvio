# Part 4 (supplement) — Complete audit grid for a product page

Part 4 laid out the principles of conversion. Here is a detailed audit grid, inspired by the public work of the Nielsen Norman Group and the Baymard Institute on the online shopping experience, to apply systematically to every product page before it goes live for good. This grid is deliberately demanding — the goal isn't to tick each box quickly, but to honestly check each point on a real page.

## Visuals: what happens in the first three seconds

The first visible image should show the product in a real usage context, not just isolated on a white background — a white background has its place in the gallery, but rarely in first position, because it doesn't answer the question the visitor is unconsciously asking: "what does it look like when I'm using it". The following images should cover the angles that answer the most common silent objections for this kind of product — size compared with an everyday object, texture or material in close-up, use in different situations if the product lends itself to it. A short video, when possible, almost always reduces uncertainty more effectively than an additional image, especially for any product whose use isn't obvious at first glance.

## The copy: answering questions before they're asked

Good product page copy isn't a list of technical specs copied from the supplier's listing — it's an anticipated answer to the concrete questions a visitor asks before buying. Who is it for, in what situation does it solve a problem, what sets it apart from a more conventional alternative, what are the product's honest limits. That last question often surprises beginner sellers, who fear that mentioning a limitation will scare the customer away — it's almost always the opposite: a page that honestly acknowledges a minor weakness ("this model is less suitable if...") gains credibility on everything else in the text, while a page presenting only perfect advantages triggers instinctive distrust in a visitor used to online marketing.

## The price and how it's presented

The price must be immediately visible, with no mental arithmetic required for the visitor to understand what they'll actually pay once shipping is added — a bad surprise on the total at checkout is one of the best-documented causes of cart abandonment. If a strikethrough price strategy is used, the struck-out reference must remain credible: a strikethrough price obviously inflated artificially, immediately perceived as such by a moderately savvy visitor, destroys trust across the whole store and not just on that page.

## Reassurance: place it where doubt appears, not just at the bottom of the page

Reassurance elements — estimated delivery time, return policy, accepted payment methods, any guarantee — are often relegated to the very bottom of the product page, in an area many visitors never see because they've already left the page before getting there. These elements are more effective when visible right next to the buy button, exactly when doubt is most likely to appear, rather than somewhere the visitor has to actively search for them.

## Reviews: give them a place, but an honest one

Visible customer reviews increase trust almost systematically, but how they're presented matters as much as their existence. Reviews that are too uniformly perfect, without the slightest nuance, raise the same suspicion as copy that's too perfect — a mix of very positive reviews and a few more measured ones, as long as none is truly negative on a deal-breaking point, generally looks more authentic than a perfect rating displayed without any nuance.

## The final test: does the page answer without anyone having to ask?

The most revealing exercise is to ask someone outside the business to read the page and say out loud every question they still have before being ready to buy. Every question asked at that moment is a question the page should have answered itself. ## Summary grid

First image in a real usage context rather than isolated. Angles covering the most likely silent objections. Copy organized around the visitor's questions rather than raw technical specs. At least one honest limitation acknowledged in the copy. Total price actually paid visible with no hidden calculation. Credible strikethrough price if one is used. Reassurance placed near the buy button, not only at the bottom of the page. Reviews presented with realistic nuance rather than suspicious perfection.
