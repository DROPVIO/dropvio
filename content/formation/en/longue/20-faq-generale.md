# Part 20 — Frequently asked questions

This part brings together the questions that come up most often once the principles of this course are in place, with answers that point, where relevant, to the part where the topic is covered in detail.

## Do you need a big starting budget?

No, and that's precisely one of the reasons dropshipping exists as a model — not having stock to finance considerably reduces the starting stake needed compared with a traditional business. The realistic minimum budget depends mostly on the ad test budget you can afford to lose without endangering your personal situation, in line with the prudent tier logic of Part 6, plus the administrative costs of creating the business mentioned in Part 9.

## How long before you know whether a product works?

There's no universal timeframe, because it depends directly on the volume of traffic and budget committed to the test. What matters isn't a fixed duration, but enough data volume to draw a reliable conclusion, as detailed in Part 6 — judging a product on the very first hours or days, in either direction, is the most common mistake at this stage.

## Can you do dropshipping without a company, just as an individual?

No, a regular commercial activity must be declared from the first euro of profit, whatever the amount, as recalled in Part 9. The micro-enterprise status remains the simplest and fastest solution to set up to start solo.

## How do you know whether a supplier is reliable before committing?

No public data guarantees 100% reliability, but comparing several suppliers yourself on their average rating and volume of orders already placed gives a solid first indication. This comparison must always be followed by a test order before any volume commitment, as detailed in Part 5 and its evaluation grid.

## What should you do if a product that sold well starts selling less?

Before concluding that the product is "exhausted", check each component of the margin and conversion calculation separately: has acquisition cost risen on that specific channel, has the return rate changed, has the product page been modified recently, has a competitor appeared on the same product with a more aggressive price. A slowdown almost always has an identifiable cause among these elements, rather than an unexplained fate of the product itself.

## Should you specialize in a single product category or diversify right away?

Part 10 recommends gradual rather than immediate diversification — first focus on validating a first product and its coherent range, before expanding into other categories. Diversifying too early, before consolidating a first base, scatters attention and budget without anything really being validated yet.

## Is dropshipping still a viable opportunity today, with so much competition?

The market has indeed changed a lot since the days when simply doing dropshipping was enough to stand out. What remains true, however, is that the method described throughout this course — rigorous margin calculation, verified suppliers, careful customer service, prudent financial management — continues to separate the stores that last from those that close, regardless of the general level of competition in the market. Competition has increased; the need for method has increased with it, rather than disappearing in favor of a model that has become impossible.
