# Part 1 — Dropshipping fundamentals and market reality

## What dropshipping really is

Before talking about products, advertising or margins, you need to be clear about the mechanism itself, because most people who start have only a rough picture of it, along the lines of "I sell stuff without stock and the money comes in by itself". That's true on one technical point and false on everything else.

The technical mechanism is simple. You never hold the product. A customer orders on your store, you collect the selling price, you pass that order on to a supplier (most often based in China, through platforms like AliExpress) at the supplier price, and that supplier ships directly to the customer. Your margin is the difference between what the customer paid and what you paid the supplier, minus all the costs around it: advertising, transaction fees, returns, VAT, your e-commerce platform subscription.

What's false in the popular picture is the idea that this technical simplicity removes the work. It removes one cost (stock) and one risk (unsold inventory), but it doesn't remove your legal responsibility toward your customer, nor the need to find a product people really want, nor the need to manage cash flow, nor the obligation to answer a customer who received a broken item when you never touched that parcel. You're an intermediary, but in the eyes of the customer and the law, you are the seller. The whole course starts from that point: dropshipping removes one barrier to entry (stock capital), it doesn't remove the others.

## The numbers they don't show you in "get rich with dropshipping" ads

There's a mountain of online content selling dropshipping as a sure-fire system. The real numbers tell a more nuanced story, and it's better to know them before you start than to discover them after spending your ad budget.

One figure appears everywhere in the industry: between 80 and 90% of dropshipping stores supposedly shut down within their first year. No verifiable primary study confirms it precisely (see Part 21), so read it as an order of magnitude, not an exact statistic — but the trend it describes is consistent with every available account from sellers. It's not because the model is broken, it's because most people who start make the same avoidable mistakes: they pick a product without a method, they never calculate their real margin before launching an ad campaign, they have no cash cushion to absorb a hard month, and they confuse revenue with profit until the day they have to pay their charges.

Among the stores that survive, only a minority become truly profitable. Industry content puts the share of stores exceeding $50,000 in monthly revenue at around 1.5% — again, a widely repeated order of magnitude rather than verified data. It's not a figure to aim for, nor a discouraging one in itself: most businesses that last stay well below that peak, and no level of income is guaranteed, however serious the method. But it puts into perspective what "succeeding at dropshipping" really means: it's almost never the viral story of the kid who becomes a millionaire in three months, it's most often a serious business, run with method over several months, that progresses slowly and with no guaranteed result.

When sellers themselves are asked what made their store fail or what most threatens their business, two answers come out far ahead in the surveys published by the industry: delivery times that are too long (around 64% of sellers surveyed) and unreliable suppliers who change their prices, stock or product quality without warning (around 48%) — percentages to take as indications, not verified measurements. These two points are not anecdotal; they'll come back constantly in this course, because they're directly tied to customer satisfaction, and therefore to your reputation, your return rates, and ultimately your profitability.

## The real profile of someone who makes it

There's no single profile of success, but there are traits that keep coming back in those who last. The first is the ability to treat this business as a company and not as a bet. A company has tracked accounts, monitored cash flow, decisions made on numbers rather than intuition or a product that "looks cool". A bet is putting an ad budget on a product seen on TikTok and hoping it takes off.

The second trait is patience with the calendar. Most stores that end up working well took several months to find the right product, the right offer, the right ad message. If you give up after two weeks because the first product doesn't work, you haven't actually tested anything — you've just confirmed that finding a winning product on the first try is rare, which was already known.

The third trait, less intuitive, is attention to what happens after the sale: customer service, handling returns, communicating when there's a delay. That's where most sellers relax their attention, when it's exactly the moment that decides the store's reputation and the dispute rate that can, in serious cases, get a payment account closed.

## The market in 2026: more demanding, not closed

Dropshipping is no longer the wild west it once was. Customers have seen hundreds of stores with the same products, the same generic visuals, the same promises of 3-5 day delivery that turn into three weeks of waiting. Their distrust has grown, and with it their expectations: they look at reviews, how clear the return policy is, how consistent the brand is, before they take out their credit card.

That's not bad news, it's a filter. It eliminates part of the competition that keeps working the old way — copy-pasted product, generic page, no customer service replies — and it rewards those who take the time to build something credible: a real selection of products that fit together, a store that inspires trust, a clear policy on delivery times and returns, a calculated margin that lets you survive a hard month rather than live order to order.
