# Part 8 — Finances and cash flow: staying stable

## Why a store that's profitable on paper can still collapse

It's one of the least understood paradoxes of e-commerce: a business can be profitable in accounting terms — sales do exceed costs over the year — and yet find itself unable to pay a bill at a given moment, simply because the money that should come in hasn't arrived yet when the money has to go out. That's a cash-flow problem, distinct from a profitability problem, and it's one of the most unfair causes of closure there is: shutting down a business that worked, for failing to anticipate a simple timing gap between receipts and payments.

## Understanding the gap between collecting and spending

In dropshipping, this gap takes a particular form. You generally collect the customer's payment immediately when the order is placed, but you pay the supplier almost right after to trigger shipping — so far, the gap works in your favor rather than against you. The real cash-flow risk appears elsewhere: in the time some payment processors take to actually pay out the funds collected, in refunds that sometimes go out several weeks after the initial payment, and above all in the temptation to immediately reinvest every euro of revenue into advertising without keeping a reserve to absorb these gaps.

## The rule of the safety reserve

A simple financial management practice, but one most beginner sellers ignore, is to always set aside a reserve equivalent to several weeks of fixed costs — subscriptions, the share of upcoming social contributions, a minimum operating budget — rather than considering that all the money available in the account can be reinvested in advertising. This reserve isn't money sitting idle; it's insurance against months when sales slow down, when a supplier causes a costly problem to solve, or when several refunds arrive at the same time.

## Separate personal money from business money, no exceptions

Mixing personal and business finances is a source of confusion that keeps you from seeing clearly whether the business is really profitable, and considerably complicates your tax return when the time comes. Even as a micro-entrepreneur, where the legal structure is simple and doesn't legally separate business and personal assets the way a company does, opening a bank account dedicated to the business and running only the store's flows through it remains a discipline that changes everything in terms of clarity. Without this separation, it becomes almost impossible to answer a question as simple as "how much did my business really make me this month", because personal and business flows mix in the same balance.

## Anticipate contributions rather than discovering them at period end

As a micro-entrepreneur, social contributions are calculated on revenue collected, generally declared and paid monthly or quarterly as chosen when the business is created. The classic mistake is to spend all receipts as they come in, without setting aside the share that will be due at declaration time — which turns a perfectly predictable deadline into a bad surprise. Setting that share aside as soon as money comes in, rather than waiting for the deadline to improvise, avoids this recurring trap.

## Track your real indicators rather than relying on an impression

A business is steered by indicators tracked regularly, not by a general impression that "it seems to be going well" or "things seem complicated right now". The indicators that matter most are the real net margin per product, already detailed in the part on pricing, the acquisition cost per customer relative to that margin, and the rate of returns and refunds that directly eats into a period's apparent profit. It's by cross-checking these indicators, rather than looking at revenue alone, that you spot in time that a product that seemed to perform well is actually becoming loss-making because of a return rate that has gradually risen without anyone noticing.

## What financial stability really changes over time

A business that keeps its cash flow in check and tracks its real indicators can absorb a bad month without calling its entire existence into question. A business living day to day, without a reserve or tracking, turns the slightest unforeseen event — a disappointing supplier, an ad campaign that suddenly converts less well, a spike in refunds on a product — into an existential threat. The difference between these two stores is almost never the quality of the product sold; it's almost always the rigor of the financial management standing silently behind it, far from the more visible and exciting aspects of the job like choosing products or creating content.
