# Part 2 (supplement) — Full case study: from idea to final choice

Part 2 laid out the criteria. Here's how they fit together in practice, through an end-to-end example — from a vague idea to a reasoned launch decision. The figures are teaching examples, not data measured on a real product; what matters is the method, reproducible on any idea you have.

## The starting point: an idea that's too broad

It usually all starts with an idea too vague to be useful as is — "something for the kitchen" or "a sports accessory". That breadth is normal at the start, but it must narrow quickly, otherwise the research that follows stays too diffuse to be usable. Narrowing means specifying a use, a moment, a type of user: not "a kitchen accessory" but "an accessory that solves a small, specific problem encountered while preparing a quick weekday meal".

## First filter: the volume and search zone

Once the idea is narrowed into three or four concrete product variants, the first step is to check, for each, whether it falls in the intermediate zone described in Part 2: neither a negligible search volume signaling no demand, nor a massive volume signaling competition that's already established and expensive to fight. A variant with modest search volume but steady growth over several months is almost always preferable to a variant with already huge but stagnant or declining volume — the second has probably already reached, or passed, its peak of opportunity.

## Second filter: the trend over time, not just the snapshot

A variant can have decent search volume at the moment you look at it, without that saying anything about its trajectory. Watching the evolution over several months, rather than a single snapshot, lets you tell apart three very different situations that can look alike in an isolated snapshot: an underlying trend that rises steadily year after year, a seasonality that returns every year at the same periods with no underlying trend, and a one-off spike tied to a fad that will probably come down as fast as it went up. The first two are exploitable with the right timing strategy; the third is the riskiest long term, even if it can be profitable very short term for someone who knows how to anticipate it and get out in time.

## Third filter: checking the supplier before any financial commitment

Once one or two variants pass the first two filters, the next step is to identify several potential suppliers for that exact product, never just one by default. Compare several potential suppliers for the same product on reliability criteria — product rating, volume of orders already placed through that supplier — rather than committing to the first result that comes up. For each candidate supplier, you need to check that the price actually charged, once converted into your reference currency, leaves a margin consistent with the calculations in Part 3 — a product that looks interesting on paper can become non-viable once the real supplier cost is confirmed.

## Fourth filter: the complementarity test with the existing offer

Finally, before validating the final choice, ask yourself whether this product fits into a range consistent with what already exists in the store, or with what could reasonably be added next. A product excellent in isolation but that doesn't relate to any other product in the store limits the cross-selling opportunities detailed in Part 4, and complicates building the coherent store identity mentioned in Part 10. A product a little less spectacular in isolation, but which naturally fits with two or three other items already in place, can be worth more to the business as a whole.

## The final decision: document, don't just decide

The last step, often forgotten, is to document why this product was chosen over another — which volume and trend figures weighed in the decision, which supplier was chosen and why, what net margin was calculated before launch. This written record seems worthless when you make it, but becomes precious several months later, when you need to understand why a product that seemed promising ended up underperforming, or on the contrary to reproduce the method that made a product succeed. Without that record, every new product choice starts from scratch on the intuition of the moment, without building on experience already accumulated.

## Summary as a checklist

Idea narrowed to a precise use rather than left vague. Search volume checked in the intermediate zone rather than at the extremes. Trend observed over several months rather than in a snapshot. Several suppliers compared on reliability before any commitment. Net margin recalculated with the confirmed real supplier price. Consistency with the existing or planned range checked. Decision documented with the reasons behind it.
