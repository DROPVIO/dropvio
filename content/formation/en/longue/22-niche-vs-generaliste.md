# Part 22 — Niche store or general store: a structuring choice

One choice comes early in a store's life and durably shapes what follows: focusing on a tight, coherent niche, or offering a broader, general range. This choice has no universal right answer, but it deserves to be made consciously rather than resulting from a pile of one-off decisions with no overall vision.

## What a niche store makes easier to build

A store focused on a specific product category, serving a well-identified customer profile, makes it possible to build more natural range coherence for the cross-selling detailed in Part 4, a more readable brand identity as mentioned in Part 10, and a stronger perceived expertise in the customer's eyes, which sets a specialized store apart from an anonymous general store among thousands of others offering the same products with no apparent link between them. This coherence also makes ad targeting easier, since an audience defined by a specific niche is generally simpler to describe and reach than a generic one.

## What a niche imposes as a constraint

On the other hand, a niche store depends more on the long-term vitality of that specific niche — a category in decline, an interest that moves elsewhere, affects the whole store rather than just one product line among others. It's the same reasoning as the one developed in Part 10 on diversification, applied this time to the scale of the entire store rather than a single star product.

## What a general store offers in resilience, and what it sacrifices in coherence

A general store, by diversifying its product categories, spreads its risk more widely against changes in a specific trend or category. In return, it sacrifices part of the coherence that facilitates cross-selling and building a recognizable identity — a visitor discovering a store selling kitchen accessories, sports equipment and tech gadgets with no apparent link perceives a clear positioning less easily, which can reduce immediate trust compared with a clearly specialized store.

## An often overlooked middle path

Between these two extremes lies an intermediate approach, sometimes more relevant than either taken alone: specializing around a use or a customer profile rather than a single strict product category — for example, a store organized around the needs of a certain lifestyle, able to include products from technically different categories but brought together by a real rather than artificial coherence of use. This approach keeps part of a niche's readability while reducing dependence on a single product category.

## The most useful decision criterion

Rather than settling this choice on an abstract preference, the most useful criterion remains to observe, once the first products have been tested using the method in Part 2, which direction the real data encourages: which products convert best, which customer profiles come back most often, which product combinations work naturally in cross-selling. A store's positioning often benefits from being refined gradually based on this real data, rather than being arbitrarily fixed on day one on the sole basis of a starting intuition.
